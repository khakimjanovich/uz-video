Saturday afternoon. Aziz steps into a lift with his phone in his hand, and the MyID screen of the mobile bank is open. Between the fourth and the fifth floor, his phone loses the network. One second earlier, MyID finished the check of his face. So did the check pass, or did it vanish? An hour ago, Aziz said yes on the consent screen, and the platform wrote a record that permits one session at MyID. That one session is the story of this hour: who opens it, what comes back, and what happens when something breaks on the way. Go back to the moment after his yes. Before the MyID screen opens, the mobile bank checks the phone itself. It looks for root access, and for signs of an emulator. On such a phone, the app asks for no session and starts no MyID check. It shows that the check is not possible on this phone. This check belongs to the app, because the platform and MyID cannot see the phone itself. Aziz's phone is clean, so the app asks the platform for a session. Only the mobile bank can ask. The bank's team adds the MyID part to the master app, and to no other app. Only one app holds the camera that checks faces against state records, so only one app must be defended. Now the backend opens the session, not the app. First, the platform looks at his login. It must have a SIM match, like his login on Monday on the mobile network. Without it, the platform calls nobody, counts nothing, and asks Aziz to log in again on the mobile network. His login has the match. The platform calls MyID with the credentials of the installation, which never go to the phone. It sends the bank's minimum face match, a configured value. It gets back a session identifier, and gives the mobile bank on this same phone what it needs to start the MyID screen. This is the proofing session, and it is now Open. It belongs to one customer, one device and one purpose: the step-up. It lives for ten minutes, a value that MyID sets. While it is open, no second session can open for Aziz. A request from a different phone gets a no, and the same phone gets back the same open session. Inside the mobile bank, the MyID screen captures his document and his face. MyID checks that the face is live, and compares the face and the document with the state records. Then it gives the app one thing: a one-time result code. The result code is not the result. It is a ticket that only the platform can exchange, within five minutes, one time. The app sends the code to the platform. The platform accepts it only from the device and the customer of the session, only while the session is open, and only once. Then it calls MyID, exchanges the code, and gets the result. Now suppose the code was copied on the way. Someone sends it again, from a different phone. The platform refuses it: wrong device, or code already used. It writes a security event. And it never stores the result code, so there is nothing to steal later. On success, the platform checks every field before it keeps it. The personal identification number must have fourteen digits. A person below the configured minimum age stays at level one, even when MyID says success, and that attempt is not counted. A document with an expiry date in the past is a failure. A face match below the bank's minimum is a failure too: face not matched. Failures get plain words. Suppose a lamp makes a glare on his document. MyID gives a reason code, and the platform turns it into plain words: poor image. Aziz reads: try again now, without the glare. He never sees a code or a provider name. Each failure also meets the limits. The platform counts failed attempts for the phone number, the device, and the personal identification number when it is known. A failure that the person or the capture caused counts, like a poor image or a face that does not match. A failure that the service caused does not count. When MyID is down, Aziz reads that the service is temporarily not available, his level does not move, and he can try again later. After the first counted failure, a cool-down starts, and after the second, a second cool-down. At the daily cap, no attempt is possible until the next day. The bank configures the cap and the cool-downs. An operator can reset the counters, for example for a phone that two customers share, and a second operator must approve the reset. And each new try starts again at the consent screen, because one yes opens one session. Back to the lift. When the network comes back, the app reports that the MyID screen ended, but no code reached the platform. The platform asks MyID for the status of the session at once. A code is there, so the exchange continues, and the check did not vanish. If MyID were still busy, the platform would ask again after the session closes. If no code were there, the attempt would not count, and Aziz could start again at once. The result is valid, so the platform keeps his verified identity, and the session is Succeeded. Aziz sees his names, his date of birth, the type of his document, the last four characters of its number, and the first two and last two digits of his personal identification number. He confirms them. Now a check for you. In the lift, the code never reached the platform: does Aziz lose an attempt? No: the platform asks MyID for the status, finds the code, and continues, and with no code the attempt would not count. Only the mobile bank runs MyID, and only after the root and emulator check. The platform opens the session, with credentials that stay in the bank. The app carries back a one-use result code, and only the platform exchanges it. Every failure has plain words and a counting rule, and the bank configures the limits. Aziz has confirmed his data, but he is not at level two yet. One step is left: the phone check, and the money for his mother still waits. Lesson fourteen first shows how the platform keeps his verified identity.
