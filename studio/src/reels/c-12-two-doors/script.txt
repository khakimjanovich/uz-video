How many ways into the locked room should there be? In lesson four, the locked room had only two doors: one for buyers' browsers, and one for systems like ecom. In lesson eleven, the card frame came through the first door, and in lesson seven, ecom's calls came through the second. This lesson builds those two doors, and shows why nothing else listens. The room has two kinds of visitor. Buyers' browsers come from the internet, to type a card into the frame. Services from other products, like ecom, come from their own servers, to ask for a charge. One big entrance could serve both. But it would open every visitor's traffic, and see it all in clear text. That entrance would become the most valuable box in the bank. So the room has two narrow doors, and nothing else listens. Think of a bank: a counter for the public, and a mail slot for partners. The first door is the counter. It is the payers' door. A buyer's browser connects to it over an encrypted line. The counter opens that line with a certificate that every browser trusts. Then it looks at the request. It passes on only the card frame's few requests: show the frame, send a card, send a code. Every other request gets the answer "not found". It also limits how fast one buyer can submit cards, at the bank's chosen rate. It never limits simply loading the page. The second door is the mail slot. It is the callers' door. A caller's connection arrives sealed. On the outside of the envelope is the name of the service it wants. The door reads only that name. It never opens the envelope. It passes it on, still sealed, to the service behind it. Passing a connection along unopened is called pass-through. Only a few names pass: the card vault, the adapters, and the login server. Any other name, or no name at all, is refused. The service behind the door then checks the caller itself, with the caller's certificate and its token. So the door never needs a key to the caller's traffic. Now watch the product. The card zone has no screen of its own, so watch what each visitor meets. The buyer opens the payment page. The browser shows its padlock, because the counter's certificate is trusted. The frame loads, and the buyer types a card. Now someone tries a different address on the same door, an admin page, say. The answer is plain: not found. Nothing behind the door is named. Now a buyer, or a script, presses "Send code" again and again, very fast. The counter steps in. The frame says: too many tries too fast, try again in a minute. The session is still good after the wait. On the callers' side, a service with a name that is not on the list knocks on the mail slot. The connection simply closes. It never reaches the card vault. A listed name without a valid certificate gets past the slot, and is then refused by the service itself. On the bank's side, the team watches the card zone in monitoring. A part that stops answering raises an alert. What does this protect? Without narrow doors, one entrance would hold every caller's secrets in clear text. One break there would expose everything. With two doors, the counter sees only buyers' frame traffic. The mail slot holds nothing it can read. And the database, the keys and the logs have no door at all. That protects the cardholders. It also protects the bank's own network around the room. The bank's firewalls stay simple: two openings, each with one job. Back to the bank. A counter that serves the public a few things, and says no to the rest. A mail slot that reads only the name on the envelope. No door ever holds every caller's traffic in clear text. Next: how a caller gets its certificate.
