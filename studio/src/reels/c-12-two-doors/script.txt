Dilnoza works in the bank's operations team, and on Monday she asks Timur for a small favour. Could the card zone get one more door, just an admin page, so she can look things up without a ticket? Timur says no, kindly, and his reason is the whole design of the card zone. In lesson eleven, buyers' browsers reached the card frame, and in lesson seven, ecom's calls reached the card zone. This lesson shows the two doors they came through, and why nothing else listens. The card zone has two kinds of visitor. Buyers' browsers come from the internet, to type a card into the frame. Services from other products, like ecom, come from their own servers, to ask for a charge. One big entrance could serve both. But it would open every visitor's traffic, and see it all in clear text. That entrance would become the most valuable box in the bank. So there are two narrow doors: a counter for the public, and a mail slot for partners. The counter is the payers' door. A buyer's browser connects to it over an encrypted line. The counter opens that line with a certificate that every browser trusts. Then it passes on only the card frame's few requests: show the frame, send a card, send a code. Every other request gets the answer "not found". It also limits how fast one buyer can submit cards, at the bank's chosen rate. It never limits simply loading the page. The mail slot is the callers' door. A caller's connection arrives sealed. On the outside of the envelope is the name of the service it wants. The door reads only that name. It never opens the envelope, and passes it on, still sealed, to the service behind it. Passing a connection along unopened is called pass-through. Only a few names pass: the card vault, the adapters, and the login server. Any other name, or no name at all, is refused. The service behind the door then checks the caller itself, with the caller's certificate and its token. So the door never needs a key to anyone's traffic. Timur shows Dilnoza what each visitor meets. A buyer opens Malika's payment page. The browser shows its padlock, because the counter's certificate is trusted. The frame loads, and the buyer types a card. Then Timur tries Dilnoza's idea: an admin page address on the same door. The answer is plain: not found. Nothing behind the door is even named. Next, he presses Send code again and again, very fast, the way a script would. The counter steps in. The frame says: too many tries too fast, try again in a minute. After the wait, the session still works. On the callers' side, a service whose name is not on the list knocks on the mail slot. The connection simply closes. It never reaches the card vault. A listed name without a valid certificate gets past the slot, and is then refused by the service itself. And if any part stops answering, monitoring raises an alert for Timur's team. Here is my position: every convenient door into a card zone becomes the door an attacker uses. With two narrow doors, the counter sees only the frame's traffic. The mail slot holds nothing it can read. The database, the keys and the logs have no door at all. And the bank's firewalls stay simple: two openings, each with one job.
