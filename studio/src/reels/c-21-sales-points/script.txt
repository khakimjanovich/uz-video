May every website of an approved merchant take payments? Picture a merchant with an approved company that adds a second website: a copy of a famous brand's shop. If every website could take payments the moment it was typed in, all the bank's checks on the company would mean nothing. A market licenses the company, but every stall is still inspected on its own. A merchant's websites work the same way. Here is how it works. Each website is a sales point: one place where buyers pay. It starts as a draft, and a draft cannot take money. When a payment ends, the buyer is sent back to the shop. Where they go is a return address. Every return address must be secure, and on the website's own site. So a payment can never send a buyer somewhere else, like a fake thank-you page. Return addresses can change only while the website is still a draft. Next, a bank admin checks the site, and records a site check: passed or failed. A failed check must say what is wrong. Then the deal. It says which cards the website takes. It can also say how much it may take per payment, and per day. Only with an active merchant, a passed check and at least one card can the website be switched on. Each card still needs its own terminal and its own fee before its payments go through. And before taking any money, every part of the system asks one question: may this website take a payment right now? The answer is yes only when the website and its merchant are both active. Now watch the product. The viewer signs in as the merchant and opens Websites. The page says each website that takes payments is checked and switched on by the bank. They click Add website. They give it a name, the website's address, and return addresses on that same site, pressing Enter after each one. Saved, the page says the bank will check it. Now the viewer tries a return address on somebody else's site. The server refuses it, and the screen shows why. Next, the bank admin portal. The admin opens the merchant and this website. A card at the top says "Before it takes payments", with a list of steps and how many are left. The Switch on button is grey. A line explains: switching on needs an active merchant, a passed check and chosen cards. The admin clicks Record a check, chooses Passed, and saves. Then Deal terms. They tick one card. The boxes "Most per payment" and "Most per day" can stay empty, which means no limit. Three steps now show done, and the Switch on button lights up. One click. A warning appears: some cards still have no terminal or fee, and their payments are refused until they do. What does this protect? Without a check on each website, a copycat shop could ride on an approved company. Without strict return addresses, a fraudster could use the bank's own payment page to send buyers to a fake page. The site check protects buyers and the bank's name. The single question asked before any money moves means a suspended website or merchant stops at once, everywhere. So, like a market stall: the company is licensed, and every website is still inspected on its own. Next: how the bank's fee is decided.
