A new bank signs the contract on a Monday. Somewhere in that bank, a platform team gets two new Kubernetes clusters, one in the test zone and one in the production zone. Between that Monday and the day Aziz types his phone number for the first time, what has to happen? In lesson six, the configuration came from OpenTofu with no default in the code, and in lesson thirty-eight, stage and production became two installations with no route between them. This last lesson installs heeyID in a bank, and then looks back at the whole road. One rule stands over everything: one installation serves one bank, and each zone of that bank gets its own installation. There is never a second bank inside, and never a call home to the vendor. Start with the configuration. The OpenTofu configuration of the installation gives the deploy values for that zone, and the defaults for every policy and every text, in the four languages. At setup, the platform reads that catalogue and writes it as version zero, marked as the product default. If one value is missing, the setup stops with a clear error, and nothing starts half configured. Then the units. Each unit is an image with a Helm chart. The gateways, Keycloak, the workers, and the MyID and push adapters run in front. Postgres, Valkey, Kafka and Vault run behind them, and each one is a new installation for that zone. Vault makes the keys of the installation, and the bank's security team keeps the keys that unseal it. Keycloak gets one realm, with one client for each app of the bank. Then the gate. The service does not start until the bank has at least one active app with a required level. The panel shows every policy and text that is still the product default, so the bank sees what it has not decided yet. Stage goes first, against the MyID test environment. Production follows as a difference in Helm values, approved by a second person. And for the developers there is a third, smaller home: Compose on a laptop. One command starts Postgres, Valkey, Kafka, Vault in development mode, and Keycloak with a client for the mobile bank, the marketplace app and the marketplace web. It also starts a simulator for each outside service, and a seed loads the default catalogue. The acceptance suite then runs with no internet at all. So when is it done? I think a definition of done is only useful if each line can be checked. The decisions are written and accepted. The API contracts of both gateways are published. Each failure path has a test, and the app-separation test passes. Audit events reach the stream, and a simulator of the bank's monitoring reads them. The code has a review, and the release has an independent security review and a penetration test. The integration guide, the runbooks and the decision log exist. And the product manager signs off after a demo on real phones in the test zone, against the MyID test identities. Now look back at the road. Aziz registered with his phone number, a code and one password, and reached level one. He proved who he is one time, with MyID, in the master app, and reached level two. The platform linked him to the bank's records and made his phone a trusted device. His side apps asked the master app for an approval, not for his face. His sessions got a rank, and a stranger with his number met a password, a TAF verdict, and his own phone saying this was not me. His document expired and was refreshed, his account was blocked and unblocked, and every step went into a chain that nobody can rewrite. Behind him were operators with one role each, rules that need two people, and two zones that never touch. All of it runs inside one bank, in one installation. Prove once, then bind. That is heeyID.
