Thursday, six in the evening. A buyer orders roses from Malika's shop and presses Pay. The buyer's bank says yes. Then the line to the card processing centre goes quiet for forty seconds, and the yes never arrives. What should the page tell the buyer now? Timur runs the bank's card zone, and he has a rule for exactly this moment. In lesson seven, the card zone checked who was calling and agreed to charge the card. This lesson follows that charge out of the card zone, to the centre. Behind every card network sits a card processing centre: the switchboard that carries a payment to the buyer's bank. Each centre has its own protocol. Its own field names, its own long list of error codes, and its own way of saying yes. If the card zone spoke every centre's language itself, every new centre would mean changing the code that guards card numbers. Timur wants that code to change as rarely as possible. So the card zone has one adapter per centre. An adapter is a small, separate service. On one side, it speaks the centre's language. On the other side, it speaks one plain language that the card zone understands. Every adapter takes the same three requests. Charge a card. Give money back. Ask what happened to a payment. The card zone never needs to know which centre is behind the adapter. The answers come back the same way. Each centre says yes and no in hundreds of ways, and the adapter turns every answer into one of three outcomes. Approved means the money moved. Declined means the buyer's bank said no, and no money moved. Unknown means the system does not know yet. A timeout is unknown. A broken or unreadable answer is unknown. Here is Timur's rule, and I agree with him completely: unknown is never declined. Most teams get this wrong, because no is simpler to handle than maybe. On Thursday evening, reading silence as no would have told the buyer the payment failed, while their bank had already said yes. The buyer pays again, and pays twice. So every charge carries the system's own payment id, and the centre keeps that id with the payment. When the line comes back, the card zone asks the centre about exactly that payment. Asking never moves money. It only reads what happened. This is what Malika's buyer saw. After pressing Pay, the page shows a spinner and the words "Taking the payment". A short line says the buyer's bank is being asked, and that this takes a few seconds. Behind that line, the card zone hands the charge to an adapter, and the adapter talks to its centre. On most evenings, a yes comes back. The page turns to "Paid" and shows a reference number from the network. When the bank says no, the page shows "Payment declined" with a plain reason, such as "There isn't enough money on the card." But on Thursday, the centre went silent. The page did not say declined. It said "We're confirming your payment", and told the buyer not to pay again. Within minutes, the system asked the centre about that one payment id, and the page turned to Paid. Timur, meanwhile, got an alert. It named the adapter that could not reach its centre in the last few minutes. It warned that charges through that adapter fail or stay unknown. Next year, the bank joins a new card network. Timur adds one adapter, and nothing else in the card zone changes. And on that Thursday, one buyer paid for roses exactly once.
