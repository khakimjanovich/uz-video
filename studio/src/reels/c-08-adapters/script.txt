How does one system talk to card networks that each speak a different language? In lesson seven, ecom proved who it was, and the card zone agreed to charge the card. In lesson two, the charge then crossed the card network to the buyer's bank, which said yes or no. This lesson opens the last step inside the card zone: how it speaks to every processing centre. Abroad, you carry a plug adapter. Every country's socket is different. Your laptop's plug never changes. You never rewire the laptop. You just pick the right adapter. Payments have the same problem. Behind every card network sits a card processing centre: the switchboard that carries a payment to the buyer's bank. Each centre has its own protocol. It has its own field names, its own long list of error codes, and its own way of saying yes. If the card zone spoke every centre's language itself, every new centre would mean changing the code that guards card numbers. That code should change as rarely as possible. So the card zone has one adapter per centre. An adapter is a small, separate service. On one side, it speaks the centre's language. On the other side, it speaks one plain language that the card zone understands. Inside the card zone, there is one plug shape for every centre. Charge a card. Give money back. Ask what happened to a payment. The questions are the same, whichever centre answers. The card zone never needs to know which centre is behind the plug. Then come the answers. Each centre says yes and no in hundreds of ways. The adapter turns every answer into one of three outcomes: approved, declined, or unknown. Approved means the money moved. Declined means the buyer's bank said no, and no money moved. Unknown is the honest third answer. A timeout is unknown. A broken or unreadable answer is unknown. Unknown is never declined, because the money may already have moved. Picture the buyer's bank saying yes, and the answer getting lost on the way back. If the system read that silence as no, the buyer would pay again, and pay twice. So every charge carries our own payment id. The centre keeps that id with the payment. Later, the card zone can ask the centre about exactly that payment. Asking never moves money. It only reads what happened. Now watch the product. The buyer has typed a card and pressed Pay. The payment page shows a spinner and the words "Taking the payment". A short line says the buyer's bank is being asked, and that this takes a few seconds. Behind that line, the card zone hands the charge to the plug, and the adapter talks to its centre. In the usual case, a yes comes back. The page turns to "Paid" and shows a reference number from the network. If the bank says no, the page shows "Payment declined" with a plain reason, such as "There isn't enough money on the card." Now the centre goes silent. The page does not say declined. It says "We're confirming your payment", and it tells the buyer not to pay again. The result follows within minutes, once the system has asked the centre about that one payment id. On the bank's side, the team gets an alert. It names the adapter that could not reach its centre in the last few minutes. It warns that charges through that adapter fail or stay unknown. What does this protect? Without adapters, one centre's odd error code could break the code that guards every card number. Without the unknown outcome, a lost yes becomes a no, and a buyer pays twice. The adapter protects the card zone from every centre's quirks. The unknown outcome protects the buyer's money. And when the bank joins a new network, it adds a new adapter, and nothing else changes. Back to the travel plug. A fourth country, a fourth socket, a fourth adapter. The laptop stays exactly the same. One adapter per socket, and silence is never no.
