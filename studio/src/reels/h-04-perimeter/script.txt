Suppose the company that wrote heeyID closes its doors tonight. Its office is dark, and its servers are off. Tomorrow at eight, Aziz opens the mobile bank and logs in. Does it work? Lessons one and two followed Aziz on his journey, and gave the map and the words for it. This lesson shows where all of that runs. The answer is yes, and that answer is the whole design. heeyID runs inside the perimeter of the bank. The bank installs it on its own servers, and the bank operates it. That copy is called an installation, and one installation serves one bank. A second bank gets a second installation, on its own servers. Nothing in the installation calls the vendor. There is no switch at the vendor that turns it off, and no data goes home. So the dark office changes nothing for Aziz. This is the most useful promise a vendor can give a bank: at night, the vendor is not needed. The personal data stays in the bank, in the Republic of Uzbekistan. Now look at the edge of the perimeter. Every call that goes out passes one gate, the egress proxy. The proxy lets out only the host names in the configuration of the installation. The list has three names, and all three are outside the bank. The first is MyID, which checks Aziz's face and passport. Only the MyID adapter calls it, with a credential from the key store of the bank. The second is the SMS vendor, which sends the text messages. It sends its delivery reports back through the edge, to one address of the SMS adapter. The third is the push service, which wakes up the app on Aziz's phone. Because it is outside, a push carries only the type of the message and a reference. The app then gets the text from heeyID, over its own session. TAF is missing from that list. Its agent already runs inside the bank network, and the TAF adapter calls only that agent. Nobody installs anything for it. That is the whole outside world of heeyID: three names, through one gate. Inside the installation, there are three networks, one behind the other. First comes the edge, where all traffic enters. Then the application network, with the two gateways, Keycloak, the workers and the four adapters. Then the data network, with Postgres, Valkey, Kafka and Vault. The edge has no connection to the data network. A request must pass a gateway first. The bank also has two zones of its own: a test zone and a production zone. heeyID runs in both: stage in the test zone, and production in the production zone. In each zone, one Linux host runs the same units with Docker Compose. There is no network route between the two zones. Stage talks only to the MyID test service, with test identities. Production talks to the real MyID, with its own credential. Only configuration moves from stage to production, and a second person approves the move. No database content moves. And there is a guard at start-up. A production build refuses to start when its MyID host is a test host, or when test customers are allowed. Back to Aziz at eight in the morning. His login goes to the edge of his bank, then to the customer gateway, then to the records and the counters. His code comes through the TAF agent, inside the bank. Nothing on that path touches the vendor, so the dark office does not matter. Now a check: the push service is outside the bank, so what may a push to Aziz's phone carry? Only the type of the message and a reference, never his personal data. One installation for one bank, inside its perimeter, operated by the bank. No call to the vendor. Three names outside, through the egress proxy: MyID, the SMS vendor and the push service. Two zones, and the same units in each. Every installation starts from its configuration, and the MyID host is only one value in it. Lesson four shows that configuration, and why a missing value stops the start.
