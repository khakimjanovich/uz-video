Imagine that the company that wrote heeyID closes its doors tonight. Its office is dark, and its servers are off. Tomorrow at eight, Aziz opens the mobile bank and logs in. Does it work? In lesson three, the three checks touched passports and phone numbers. This lesson shows where those checks run, and the short list of places that heeyID may call. The answer is yes, and that answer is the whole design. heeyID runs inside the perimeter of the bank. The bank installs it on its own infrastructure, and the bank operates it. One installation serves one bank, with all its apps. A second bank gets a second installation, on its own infrastructure. Nothing in the installation calls the vendor. There is no control plane at the vendor, and no data that goes home. The installation does not need the vendor to work, so the dark office changes nothing for Aziz. I think this is the most useful promise a vendor can give a bank: at night, the vendor is not needed. The personal data stays in the bank, in the Republic of Uzbekistan, as the personal-data law says. Now look at the edge of the perimeter. What may cross it? The list is short, and the installation configuration names every host on it. First, the services of the bank: the SMS service, the customer records, the antifraud system and the security monitoring. Second, TAF, the antifraud service of the mobile operators. Its agent already runs inside the bank network, so heeyID calls only the API of that agent. Nobody installs anything for it. Third, MyID, outside the perimeter. heeyID calls it only from its MyID adapter, over a pinned connection, with a credential from the key store of the bank. And one more: the push service, which wakes up the app on Aziz's phone. It is outside too, so a push carries only the type of the message and a reference. It has no code, no text and no personal data. The app then gets the text from heeyID, over its own session. That is the whole list. An egress proxy at the edge lets only these names out. Inside the perimeter, the installation has zones of its own. All traffic comes in through one edge. Behind the edge are the application services: the two gateways, Keycloak and the workers. Behind them is the data zone: Postgres, Valkey, Kafka and Vault. The data zone accepts no connection from the edge. A request must pass a gateway first. Now the second kind of zone. The bank runs heeyID two times: stage in its test zone, and production in its production zone. They are the same units, with no network route between them. Stage talks only to the MyID test environment, with test identities. Production talks to the real MyID, with its own credential. Only configuration moves from stage to production, and a second person approves the move. No database content moves. And there is a guard at start-up. A production build refuses to start when its MyID host is a test host, or when test customers are allowed. Back to Aziz at eight in the morning. His login goes to the edge of his bank, then to the customer gateway, then to the records and the counters in the data zone. His code comes through the TAF agent, inside the bank. Nothing on that path leaves the country, and nothing touches the vendor. So the dark office does not matter. To recap: one installation for one bank, inside its perimeter. No call to the vendor. A short list of names outside: the services of the bank, the TAF agent, MyID and the push service. Zones inside the installation, and two installations: stage and production. But inside one installation, the mobile bank, the marketplace and the credit service all share one Keycloak. If the marketplace leaks a token, what stops that token from opening the mobile bank?
