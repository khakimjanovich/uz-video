Remember the operator who blocked Aziz on Friday night? One search, one button, one reason, and every session of a person ended at once. That is a lot of power on one screen. So who may sit at that screen, and who watches the person who does? In lesson thirty-six, every staff action became an audit event in a chain that nobody can rewrite. This lesson opens the administrator panel itself: how staff sign in, what each role can do, and why no single person can change a rule alone. Start at the door. Staff sign in only through the bank's own staff identity, with multi-factor authentication there. The platform issues no staff credentials and keeps no staff passwords. A staff session ends after the configured time with no activity, and after the configured lifetime. Behind the door is the admin gateway. It uses the same domain code as the customer gateway, so there is no second copy of the business rules to drift apart. Each staff user has exactly one of four roles. The role comes from the bank's staff identity, or from a staff-role table that a security administrator keeps. A viewer can see the customer, the devices, the proofing history, the cases, the phone reviews and the events, and nothing more. An operator acts on customers: block and unblock, revoke devices, end sessions, reset attempt counters, ask for a refresh, and decide phone reviews and cases. An operator also cancels or approves a waiting password reset, and opens a closed app account again. A security administrator proposes and approves changes to policies and provider settings, and keeps the staff-role table. Compliance manages the texts of consents, terms and notifications, and exports the audit records and the data of one customer. Notice what is missing: the security administrator cannot block Aziz, and the operator cannot change a policy. And the platform checks the role at each operation on the server, not only by hiding a button on the screen. Now the operator's screen. The operator finds a customer only with the exact verified phone number, or the exact personal identification number. There is no search by name, so nobody browses customers out of curiosity. One screen shows the state, the level, the link, the devices, the app accounts with their sessions, the proofing history, the cases and the recent events. Sensitive values are masked: the personal identification number shows its first two and last two digits, and the document only its last four characters. The panel shows no full document numbers, no addresses and no images. Each operator action needs a written reason. It starts at once, it writes an audit event with the operator's identity, and when it touches the customer, it sends the security notification. Cases from lesson thirty-four wait in one list, and phone reviews from lesson eighteen wait in another. Now the most important rule of the panel, and I think the one that saves a bank on a bad day: maker-checker. On Monday, a security administrator wants a shorter lifetime for one-time codes. The change does not happen. It becomes a proposal, which shows the current value next to the proposed one. A second security administrator opens it. The person who proposed cannot approve, so it must be someone else with the same role. The second person approves, and only then does the change start. It becomes a new version, with the proposer, the approver, the times and the reason. A rejected proposal is kept too, with its reason, and anyone with the role can propose an earlier version again. The same rule covers provider settings, consent and terms texts, notification templates, and the staff-role table. One tired person, or one stolen staff account, cannot quietly loosen a limit. To recap: staff come in through the bank's own identity, with one role each. Operators act on customers, security administrators on rules, Compliance on texts and exports, and viewers only look. Every action has a reason and an audit event, and every rule change needs two people. But everything in this panel is about real customers.
