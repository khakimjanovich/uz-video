It is Sunday morning, and Aziz opens the mobile bank to pay for his gym. A yellow line sits at the top of the screen: your passport expires soon, please refresh your identity. His face has not changed, his phone has not changed, and his password has not changed. So why does the bank care about a date in a small book in his drawer? In lesson fifteen, MyID proved Aziz with a full proofing, and in lesson seventeen the platform kept the result as a verified identity, in versions. This lesson adds time: a document ends, and the record must follow it, without one field changing behind Aziz's back. Start with the date. The verified identity keeps the expiry date of the document. From that date, the platform calculates a document status: valid, expiring or expired. Expiring starts the configured lead time before the date, and expired starts after the date. A job in the workers calculates the status again one time each day, and again at each refresh. When the status becomes expiring, the platform writes one audit event: document expiring. From then on, the bank's app asks Aziz to do a refresh at each start. That is the yellow line he sees on Sunday. Aziz is busy, so he taps later, and the date passes. Now the status is expired. What happens next is the part I like most, because of what does not happen. The access grant stays valid. The platform does not block Aziz, and it does not ask for MyID at login. Instead, the identity summary that the app reads gets a restricted flag, and a list of allowed actions that the bank configures. Aziz gets the security notification document expired, by SMS to his verified number and by push to his devices. The trail gets the audit event document expired. In this first version, the app of the bank applies the restriction from the flag, and a later phase moves that rule into the platform. Only one thing removes the flag: a successful refresh. So what is a refresh? It is not a form where someone types the new passport number. It is a new full proofing, with the purpose refresh. A refresh can start in three ways. Aziz can start it himself, for example on the day he gets his new passport. The app starts it while the status is expiring or expired. And an operator can ask for it, with a reason, and the app shows that request at the next start. Before it begins, a refresh needs a trusted device and a valid access grant. Then it takes the road from module four. The consent gate shows its text, and Aziz accepts it. The platform opens a MyID session with his personal identification number already set, so the master app goes straight to the face. Aziz looks into the camera, and MyID compares his face with the state records. The result comes back, and Aziz confirms the data on his screen. One step is missing on purpose: a refresh does not do the phone check. Now the result. The personal identification number is the same, so the platform writes a new version of the verified identity and makes it current. It keeps the earlier version. The reusable identifier and its expiry date are new too, so later re-checks can use them. The document status is calculated again: valid, and the restricted flag goes away. Aziz gets the security notification identity refreshed, and the trail gets the audit event identity refreshed. Here is the rule under all of this, and I think it is the most important sentence of the lesson. No field of the verified identity changes without a live face check that the customer completed, in a session that the platform opened. Not a support ticket, not an import from another system, not an operator with good intentions. An operator can ask for a refresh, but an operator cannot type a new surname. A refresh also obeys the attempt limits of lesson eleven, for the personal identification number and the device. Only a refresh that an operator asks for is not counted. To recap: the expiry date gives a status, and the status gives a reaction. Expiring asks, expired restricts, and neither one blocks. Only a new live face check changes the record, as a new version next to the old one. But there is one case where a refresh does end in a block: when the result carries a different personal identification number. So what does a block really do, and who is allowed to lift it?
