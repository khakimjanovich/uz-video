Monday, a quarter past eight in the morning. A buyer pays for a box of peonies, and at that exact moment, the bank's app restarts. Malika ships only when the bank tells her shop that a payment is paid. So the question is simple: will that news ever arrive? In lesson twenty-seven, a payment ended paid or declined, in lesson twenty-eight, even a silent one found its end, and in lesson one, the shop's server was told how its payment ended. This lesson makes sure that news is never lost, and never invented. The naive way takes two steps. The app saves the payment as paid, and then tells the shop. If it crashes between those steps, the shop never hears. Malika ships nothing, and the buyer has paid for nothing. Swap the order, and it is worse. The app tells the shop paid, then crashes before saving. Now Malika ships peonies for a payment that does not exist. Two separate steps can always be split by a crash. Most systems are built this way, and they work, until the one restart that lands in between. So when a payment reaches its end, such as paid or declined, its news is written to the outbox. The outbox is a tray inside the bank's own records. The news and the payment's final state are saved together, in one step. Either both are saved, or neither is. There is never a paid payment without its news, and never news without a paid payment. Next comes the relay. It keeps collecting the oldest unsent news from the outbox, and passes it to the bank's message stream. Several copies of the app may run at once. Each copy takes different news, so no two pick the same one. Every piece of news is labelled with its payment. So all the news about one payment travels in one line, always in order. Paid can never overtake created. The relay marks news as sent only after the stream confirms it has it. So consider a crash at the worst moment: after sending, but before marking. When the app starts again, it sends that news once more. That is at least once delivery. News may arrive twice, but never zero times. To make repeats harmless, every piece of news carries its own id. A reader who sees an id twice simply drops the second one. One more rule keeps this simple. The bank's records stay the truth. The stream only carries the news about it. Here is how it looks on an ordinary day. A buyer pays. In the merchant portal, the payment shows paid. Malika opens it, and the webhooks section soon shows its first piece of news, on its way to her shop's server. Now back to Monday's restart. The bank's app stopped at the exact moment the peonies were paid. It started again a minute later. The payment is still paid, and its news still goes out. Nothing is missing. On Bekzod's server, that news may now arrive twice. Both copies carry the same id. His server keeps the first and drops the second. So Malika never waits forever for news lost in a crash. She never ships because of news about a payment that never happened. And she never sees refunded before paid. The bank never has to explain news that its own records do not show.
