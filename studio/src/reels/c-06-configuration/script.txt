What should a service do when a setting is missing? Picture a payment service with a timeout written in its settings file: thirty seconds, as a sensible default. In production, nobody sets the real value, so thirty seconds quietly stays. One day the card network takes forty seconds to answer. The service gives up at thirty and tells the shop the payment failed. But the card network said yes, and the buyer's money is gone. A guessed default looked safe until production. A good cook works the other way. If an ingredient is missing, the cook stops the dish. The cook never guesses a pinch. Our services are the same. The rule is simple: no setting has a default value. Every setting comes from one locked store, Vault: a safe for settings and secrets. Three kinds of value go into it. Some belong to the system itself, like which service listens where. Some are secrets, like passwords and keys, and every one of them is generated inside Vault. Nobody types a password into a file, and the product ships none. And some only this bank knows, like the card network's address and the bank's login there. The operator types each of those once, into Vault. Before a service starts, it is handed exactly its own settings, in a private file only it can read. Its own configuration holds only the names of settings, never the values. If even one value is missing, the service refuses to start. It names every missing one at once, not just the first. That is fail fast: stop loudly at the start, instead of failing quietly later. The same rule covers the bank's business decisions. Rates, limits and waiting times are the bank's choices, so each is a setting the bank chooses, never a number hidden in the product. The card zone has its own settings too, and every one is the bank's. The bank chooses which systems may call the card zone, and which roles each caller gets. For each caller, the bank chooses which websites may show the card form, how long a card-entry session lives, and how many tries a buyer gets. The bank chooses the fraud limits: how many attempts one card, one group of cards, one address and one shop may make within its chosen time window. The bank chooses how long an unused card is kept before it is deleted, and how often the keys that lock card numbers change. And the bank chooses when the nightly clean-up jobs run. Now watch the product. Settings have no screen, so watch how the system behaves. The operator installs the card zone. Vault holds the system's values and the generated secrets, but one caller's fraud limit has not been typed in yet. The install stops before anything runs. Its message names the missing value, and the caller it belongs to. The operator types it in and runs the install again. Now take one service on its own, started with nothing set at all. It does not start. It prints one message: required settings are missing. Below it comes one line for every missing setting, by name. Nothing half-works. Nothing listens with a guessed address, a test password or a made-up timeout. Then its settings are handed over, and the service starts. What does this protect? A wrong guess can never reach production, so a payment never fails on a timeout nobody chose. A test password can never guard real money, because no password exists outside Vault. And the bank stays in charge: every limit, window and time in the product is a value the bank chose and can see. That protects the buyer's money, and the bank's control over its own system. Back to the cook. Every ingredient on the counter, or no dish at all. Like the cook, the service won't guess: every value comes from Vault, or it doesn't start.
