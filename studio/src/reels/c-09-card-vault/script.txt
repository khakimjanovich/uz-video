Once a year, Timur runs a drill he calls the worst day. He hands a colleague a full copy of the card zone's database and says: get me one card number out of this. If the table held plain card numbers, the drill would take a second. If it held numbers locked with a key kept right next to them, it would take two. Most teams stop at that second version, and I think it is the most dangerous kind of safety, because it looks finished. The key must never sit next to the data. In lesson six, Vault came in to hold every key, so no key is ever typed into a file. This lesson gives Vault a second job: sealing card numbers. When Malika's buyer types a card, the card vault, the service that keeps cards, hands the number to Vault. Vault hands back a sealed value. The card vault keeps only that sealed value. The key never leaves Vault. Each seal is tied to its own card. A sealed value copied into another card's place will not open. So nobody can shuffle values around to trick the system. Then a puzzle. Sometimes the card zone must find a card again by its number. It must do that without opening every seal. So Vault also stamps each number with a fingerprint. The same number always gives the same fingerprint. The fingerprint cannot be turned back into the number. This stamp is called a blind index. The card zone looks up the fingerprint, never the number. The database guards its own door too. It refuses any card value that is not sealed. Even a mistake in the code cannot write a plain number into it. What if Vault is down? Then the card vault stops. It never guesses, and it never falls back to keeping a plain number. Stopping safely instead of carrying on unsafely is called fail closed. A number is opened for one reason only: to hand it to the card's network for a charge. Every use of card data is written down: what happened, who asked, and when. And a card nobody has used for the bank's chosen time is removed. Only a short note keeps its token and its masked number. Malika's buyer sees none of this. The buyer types a card into the card form and confirms the code. The form says "Card accepted" and empties its number field. The payment page then shows one line: the first six digits, a row of stars, and the last four. Beside them sit the card's network and the bank that issued it. That masked line is all anyone outside the card zone ever sees. During the drill, Timur stops Vault on purpose. A buyer types a card, and the form refuses it with a short message. Nothing is saved, sealed or unsealed. Timur's team gets an alert: a service the card zone depends on is unavailable. The alert names the part that failed. He starts Vault again, and the same card goes through. Then the colleague reports back on the copy. Sealed values. Fingerprints. Masked numbers. No keys. A backup is just as useless without the Vault that sealed it, so the bank keeps both, and keeps them apart. A stolen database, a stolen backup, or a curious insider all see noise. The colleague never gets a card number, and that is the only result the drill allows. Next: what a card's first digits tell you.
