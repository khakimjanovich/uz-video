How do you stop a website taking more money than it is allowed? In lesson twenty-one, each website's deal set the most it may take per payment and per day. In lesson twenty-seven, one claim made sure a card was charged only once. This lesson makes those limits hold, even when five buyers pay at the same moment. Two things can break a limit. A website's account is taken over, and the attacker creates one payment for a fortune. Or, on a busy day, five buyers pay at once. Each payment fits the day's limit alone, but together they go far over it. A check that first reads the day's total and then charges lets all five through, because all five read the total before any of them is added. Think of a concert hall with one seat left. Two clerks sit at the counter, and two fans arrive at the same moment. The hall still sells that seat only once. Whoever comes second hears: sold out. A website's payment limits work the same way. Payment limits are the most a website may take in one payment, and the most in one day. The bank sets both for each website, as part of its deal. The first limit is simple. A payment over the most per payment is refused the moment it is created. That holds whether the merchant's server creates it, or a payment link does. The daily limit is harder, because buyers race each other. So each charge makes an atomic reservation. Atomic means one step that cannot be split. In that one step, the charge adds its amount to the day's total, but only if the new total still fits under the limit. When two charges arrive together, one waits until the other is done. The one that no longer fits fails before any money moves. The reservation is saved together with the charge's own record, before the card network is ever asked. If the card is declined, its share goes back to the day. A reversal on the same day gives it back too. A payment whose outcome is unknown keeps its share until someone knows what happened. And the day is the same business day the daily reports add up. In front of all of this stands the edge: the first server every request meets when it arrives from the internet. It accepts only modern encrypted connections. It tells browsers to use secure connections only. It never names the software it runs. It refuses requests that are too large, and it limits how fast one address may call. Let's watch the product. A bank admin opens a website in the bank's portal and goes to its deal terms. Two fields sit side by side: Most per payment, and Most per day. A hint says that empty means no limit. The admin enters the bank's chosen amounts and saves. In the merchant's portal, the website now shows its limits: so much a payment, so much a day. The merchant creates a payment link for an amount above the per-payment limit. The portal shows a red notice: Not done. It says this website takes at most that amount in one payment. No link is created. Now picture the last minutes of a busy day. Five buyers press Pay together. Four charges fit, and go through one after another. The fifth no longer fits. Its payment fails with a plain message: the website has reached its limit for today, and no money was taken. What does this protect? It protects the bank from a stolen account that tries to move a fortune in one payment. It protects the merchant from a busy day that quietly goes over the deal. And it protects buyers: when a limit stops a payment, it stops it before their card is charged, never after. Back to the concert hall. However many clerks, however many fans at once, every seat is sold exactly once. Next: how the system is installed, starting with its keys.
