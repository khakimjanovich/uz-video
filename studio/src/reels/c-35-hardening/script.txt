The seventh of March, ten minutes to midnight. Tomorrow is Women's Day, and Malika's shop has never been busier. Five buyers press Pay at the same moment. Each payment fits her daily limit alone, but together they go far over it. There is a worse night too: someone steals the shop's account, and creates one payment for a fortune. In lesson twenty-one, each website's deal set the most it may take per payment and per day, and in lesson twenty-seven, one claim made sure a card was charged only once. This lesson makes those limits hold, even when five buyers pay at the same moment. Payment limits are the most a website may take in one payment, and the most in one day. The bank sets both for each website, as part of its deal. The first limit is simple. A payment over the most per payment is refused the moment it is created. That holds whether the merchant's server creates it, or a payment link does. The daily limit is harder, because buyers race each other. Most teams check it the obvious way: read the day's total, and then charge. I think that check is worse than none, because it looks safe. All five charges read the total before any of them is added, so all five go through. So here, each charge makes an atomic reservation. Atomic means one step that cannot be split. In that one step, the charge adds its amount to the day's total, but only if the new total still fits under the limit. When two charges arrive together, one waits until the other is done. The one that no longer fits fails before any money moves. The reservation is saved together with the charge's own record, before the card network is ever asked. If the card is declined, its share goes back to the day. A reversal on the same day gives it back too. A payment whose outcome is unknown keeps its share until someone knows what happened. And the day is the same business day the daily reports add up. In front of all of this stands the edge: the first server every request meets when it arrives from the internet. It accepts only modern encrypted connections. It tells browsers to use secure connections only. It never names the software it runs. It refuses requests that are too large, and it limits how fast one address may call. A week before the holiday, Dilnoza opens Malika's website in the bank's portal and goes to its deal terms. Two fields sit side by side: Most per payment, and Most per day. A hint says that empty means no limit. She enters the bank's chosen amounts and saves. In Malika's portal, the website now shows its limits: so much a payment, so much a day. Bekzod tries a payment link for an amount above the per payment limit. The portal shows a red notice: Not done. It says this website takes at most that amount in one payment. No link is created. Now the last minutes before midnight. Five buyers press Pay together. Four charges fit, and go through one after another. The fifth no longer fits. Its payment fails with a plain message: the website has reached its limit for today, and no money was taken. The stolen account cannot move a fortune in one payment. The busy night cannot quietly go over the deal. And when a limit stops a buyer, it stops them before their card is charged, never after.
