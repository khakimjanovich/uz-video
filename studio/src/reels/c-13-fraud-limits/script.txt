Sunday, three in the morning. Malika is asleep, and a script starts firing card numbers at her shop's payment page. Somebody has bought ten thousand stolen numbers. Most of them are dead. To find the live ones, they try them on a small shop, where nobody is watching at three in the morning. That is called card testing. Every try reaches a card network. Every try may text a code to a real person's phone. So card testing costs the bank money, floods strangers with codes, and hands the thief a list of working cards. In lesson nine, each card got a fingerprint, and in lesson ten, its first digits named its network. This lesson turns both into counters. Each payment session already allows only a few tries. When they run out, the session ends. But the thief just opens a new session. Most teams stop at that limit per session, and I think it is nearly useless against a script. Locking a door after three wrong keys means nothing when the thief simply walks to the next door. So the card zone counts the keys, not the doors. It writes every attempt down before any network is asked. Attempts made at the same moment count each other, so speed does not help the thief. Each attempt is counted four ways. By the card, through its fingerprint, never its number. By the card's first digits. By the buyer's address on the internet. And by the shop. For each of the four, the bank chooses a limit and a time window. So many failed tries per card, per first digits, per address and per shop, within that window. A limit on tries within a time window is called a velocity limit. Past any one of the four limits, a new try is refused before any network is asked. No code is sent. No attempt is spent. And no new session opens for that shop until the window passes. Only failures that say something about the card count against it. A wrong code counts. A card the buyer's bank refuses counts. A mistyped expiry date does not hold back a whole group of cards. And the card zone's own failures, such as a network being down, never count against anyone. When refusals pile up, an alert goes out to the team. On Saturday afternoon, a real buyer had a bad day on the same page. They typed the wrong code. The form said the code was wrong, and how many tries were left. They ran out of tries. The form cleared, and the payment page showed a yellow note: that attempt ended, too many tries or the time ran out, enter the card again. A fresh, empty form appeared, and the buyer paid. At three on Sunday, the script met a different page. For a while, each card was simply refused. Then a counter reached its limit. From that moment, the form answered every new card the same way: "Too many unsuccessful attempts. Try again later." No code was sent. No network was asked. No stranger's phone buzzed. At the bank, an alert email reached Timur's team. It said card entry was refused by a velocity rule. It named the caller, and the counter that tripped: the card, the first digits, the address, or the shop. It told them where to look next: the logs, and the buyer addresses behind the refusals. Malika woke up to ordinary Sunday orders. Her page never became a thief's testing bench. Strangers' phones stayed quiet. And the bank paid for no tries, and handed out no list of live cards.
