What may you keep about a card? When your bank calls you, they say: your card ending seven eight nine zero. They never read out the whole number. And they never ask for the three digits on the back. Now picture a support engineer at a shop. A payment failed, so the engineer pastes the buyer's whole card number into a support ticket, to ask the bank about it. The number now lives in the ticket. It lives in the ticket's backups, in its search, and with everyone who can read tickets. One paste has copied a card number into many places, and none of them was built to guard it. Systems should talk about cards the way your bank does on the phone. Here are the words for that. The long number on the front of the card is the PAN, the card's own number. It is the one piece a thief needs most. In this system it lives in one place only: the card zone, the locked room from the first lesson. The three digits on the back are the card's security code. The card industry's rules say nobody may keep it once a payment is approved. This system goes further: it never even asks for it. When screens and reports need to show a card, they show the masked number. That is the first six digits and the last four, with stars between. The first six digits only tell which bank gave out the card. The last four let a person recognise their own card. Nothing in between is shown, so the masked number cannot be used to pay. And when another system needs to point at a card, to charge it later, it keeps a card token. A card token is a random name the card zone gives the card. Nothing in it comes from the number. Make two tokens, and they share nothing. So a token tells a thief nothing about the card. Three words, three rules. The PAN lives only in the card zone. The masked number may be shown. The token may be kept. And the code on the back is never kept by anyone. Now watch the product. A buyer opens the bank's payment page. Under "Card details" sits the card form. It asks for the number and the date on the card. There is no box for the code on the back. The buyer types the card and continues. The page now shows the card as one short line: the masked number, the name of the buyer's bank, and its card network. The full number is gone from the screen. Now open the bank admin portal and its Payments page. Each payment has a Card column. Every row shows a masked number: six digits, stars, four digits. Nowhere in the portal is there a full card number. The bank's own staff see exactly what your bank says on the phone, and no more. What does this protect? A masked number on a screen leaks nothing useful. A token in a database leaks nothing either, because it is only a random name. The PAN, if it leaks, lets a thief try to spend the buyer's money. So the PAN stays in one room, and everything else gets a smaller, safer word. This protects the buyer first, and then the shop and the bank, who would pay for the leak. Back to the support ticket. It should have held the masked number, or the token, and never the PAN. With either one, the bank can still find the payment, and the ticket leaks nothing. Your bank never asks for the code on the back, and our systems never hold the PAN: they hold the masked number and the token. Next: where a card number may live.
