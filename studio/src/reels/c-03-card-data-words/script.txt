Tuesday, ten in the morning. A buyer writes to Malika: my payment failed, here is my card. The message holds all sixteen digits. Malika forwards it to Bekzod, the developer who connects her shop to the bank. Bekzod wants to help, so he pastes the whole number into a support ticket to the bank. In lesson two, that number was all the buyer's bank needed to say yes, so whoever holds it can pay with it. This lesson gives you the words that let a system talk about a card without ever pasting its number. The number now lives in the ticket. It lives in the ticket's backups, in its search, and with everyone who can read tickets. One paste copied a card number into places never built to guard it. Dilnoza, in the bank's operations team, opens the ticket. She replies in one line: never send this again, the last four digits are enough. She talks the way your own bank does on the phone: your card ending seven eight nine zero. A bank never reads out the whole number. And it never asks for the three digits on the back. I think the most dangerous place for a card number is not a hacked server. It is a helpful person with a text box. Here are the words. The long number on the front of the card is the PAN, the card's own number. It is the one piece a thief needs most. In this system it lives in one place only: the card zone, the locked room from lesson one. The three digits on the back are the card's security code. The card industry's rules say nobody may keep it once a payment is approved. This system goes further: it never even asks for it. When screens and reports show a card, they show the masked number. That is the first six digits and the last four, with stars between. The first six only tell which bank gave out the card. The last four let a person recognise their own card. Nothing in between is shown, so the masked number cannot be used to pay. And when another system needs to point at a card, to charge it later, it keeps a card token. A card token is a random name the card zone gives the card. Nothing in it comes from the number. Make two tokens, and they share nothing. So a stolen token tells a thief nothing about the card. Three words, three rules. The PAN lives only in the card zone. The masked number may be shown. The token may be kept. And the code on the back is kept by no one. Malika's buyer met those rules on the bank's payment page. Under "Card details" sits the card form. It asks for the number and the date on the card. There is no box for the code on the back. The buyer types the card and continues. The page now shows one short line: the masked number, the buyer's bank and its card network. Dilnoza opens the bank admin portal, on its Payments page. Each payment has a Card column. Every row shows a masked number: six digits, stars, four digits. Nowhere in the portal is there a full card number. So Dilnoza never needed the PAN. The masked number and the amount are enough to find the payment. A leaked mask or a leaked token gives a thief nothing to spend. A leaked PAN lets a thief spend the buyer's money, and the shop and the bank pay for it. So the PAN stays in one room, and everything else gets a smaller, safer word. Bekzod deletes the number from the ticket. The backups keep it anyway. That is why the rule is never to paste it at all. Next: where a card number may live.
