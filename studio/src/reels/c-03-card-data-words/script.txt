What may you keep about a card? When your bank calls you, they say: your card ending seven eight nine zero. They never ask for the three digits on the back. Now picture a support engineer who pastes a whole card number into a ticket, to check it with the bank. The number now lives in the ticket's backups, in its search index, and with everyone who reads tickets. One pasted number lives everywhere the ticket goes. Systems should talk about cards the way your bank does. The long number on the front of the card is the P A N, the card's own number. It lives in one place only, the card vault, and lesson four shows why. The code on the back must never be kept once a payment is approved. And this system never even asks for it. Screens and logs may show the masked number: the first six digits and the last four, with nothing between. That is your bank's card ending seven eight nine zero. The first digits only tell which bank issued the card, and lesson ten reads them. Other systems keep a card token. A card token is a random name the card vault gives the card. Nothing in it comes from the number, so the token tells a thief nothing about the card. In lesson four, the token becomes a coat-check ticket. Now back to the support ticket. It should have held the masked number or the token, never the P A N. Now make both yourself, the way the code does. The card vault keeps two numbers: six digits shown first, and four shown last. A token is a short prefix plus sixteen random bytes, written in hex. Open J shell, which comes with Java, and take a made-up card number. Keep the first six and the last four, and star out the rest. Then draw sixteen random bytes, and write them after the prefix. Run it again, and you get a different token, with nothing from the number in either. Your bank never asks for the code on the back, and our systems never hold the P A N: they hold the masked number and the token. Next: where a card number may live.
