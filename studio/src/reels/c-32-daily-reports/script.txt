The last day of October. Malika's accountant puts the bank's payouts next to the shop's own orders. One week is off by the price of two bouquets, and nobody can say why. Was last week's refund counted today? Was a reversed payment counted as a sale? Did one night simply not run? A total that nobody can take apart cannot be trusted. In lesson one, ecom counted at the end of each day what the shop earned, and kept the bank's fee. This lesson builds that count, night by night, so every payout can be explained. Every night, the bank counts the day for each website. Two things make that count trustworthy: a clear day, and a count you can repeat. First, the clear day. A business day runs from midnight to midnight, in the bank's own time zone. It is the same day that decides whether money goes back by reversal or by refund. So the reports and the refund rules never disagree about which day a payment belongs to. Then the count. Every night, the bank adds up each website's day in four numbers. Paid is the payments made that day that were not reversed. Refunded is the refunds made that day, whenever the original payment was. The fee is the bank's share, from the bank's fee rules. And the payout is what the merchant receives: what was paid, less what was refunded, each after its fee. A day with many refunds and few sales gives a negative payout. That is not an error. It is the honest answer for that day. Now the part you can repeat. Counting a day again is a rebuild. A rebuild replaces the day's numbers; it never adds to them. This is where I see most teams go wrong. They add each night's numbers to a running total, and the first time someone reruns a night, the merchant is paid twice. So each night, the bank counts not only yesterday, but a few days before it again. If one night is missed, the next night simply catches up. Nothing is ever counted twice. And behind every total sits a list of every line: each payment, and each refund as a negative line. So any number can be explained, one line at a time. Malika opens her portal and chooses Reports. The page is called Daily reports. It explains that each night the bank adds up the day before for every website. There is one row for each website and business day. The columns read Business day, Paid, Bank fee, Refunded, and You receive. One day shows a payment and a refund. Paid shows the payment's amount. Refunded shows the refund. You receive shows what is left after the fee. On another day, refunds outweigh sales, and You receive shows a minus. Malika downloads that week's lines for her accountant. The file opens in a spreadsheet. The payment is there, with its order, its website, its amount, its fee and its time. The refund is there too, as a negative line. The card column shows only the first six and the last four digits. Add up the lines, and you get the totals on the screen. The accountant finds it in a minute: a refund made on Monday, for a bouquet bought the week before. When Malika first joined, this page was empty. It said a report appears the morning after the first day with payments. A payout the merchant can check, a total support can take apart, and no night missed or counted twice. That is what a clear day and a rebuild buy. Next: when a buyer says, I never bought this.
