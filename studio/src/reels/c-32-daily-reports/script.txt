How much money does the merchant get today? At the end of the month, a merchant's accountant puts the bank's payouts next to the shop's own orders. One week is off, and nobody can say why. Was last week's refund counted today? Was a reversed payment counted as a sale? Did one night simply not run? A total that nobody can take apart cannot be trusted. Think of a shop at closing time. The owner counts the till for the day. Counting it again gives the same answer. Two things make that count trustworthy: a clear day, and a count you can repeat. The bank does the same, every night, for each website. First, the clear day. A business day runs from midnight to midnight, in the bank's own time zone. It is the same day that decides, in Lesson thirty-two, whether money goes back by reversal or by refund. So the reports and the refund rules never disagree about which day a payment belongs to. Then the count. Every night, the bank adds up each website's day in four numbers. Paid is the payments made that day that were not reversed. Refunded is the refunds made that day, whenever the original payment was. The fee is the bank's share, from the fee rules of Lesson twenty-two. And the payout is what the merchant receives: what was paid, less what was refunded, each after its fee. A day with many refunds and few sales gives a negative payout. That is not an error. It is the honest answer for that day. Now the part you can repeat. Counting a day again is a rebuild. A rebuild replaces the day's numbers; it never adds to them. So each night, the bank counts not only yesterday, but a few days before it again. If one night is missed, the next night simply catches up. Nothing is ever counted twice. And behind every total sits a list of every line: each payment, and each refund as a negative line. So any number can be explained, one line at a time. Let's watch the product. The merchant's admin opens the portal and chooses Reports. The page is called Daily reports. It explains that each night the bank adds up the day before for every website. There is one row for each website and business day. The columns read Business day, Paid, Bank fee, Refunded, and You receive. One day shows a payment and a refund. Paid shows the payment's amount. Refunded shows the refund. You receive shows what is left after the fee. On another day, refunds outweigh sales, and You receive shows a minus. The admin downloads that day's lines. The file opens in a spreadsheet. The payment is there, with its order, its website, its amount, its fee and its time. The refund is there too, as a negative line. The card column shows only the first six and the last four digits. Add up the lines, and you get the totals on the screen. A shop with no payments yet sees an empty page instead. It says a report appears the morning after the first day with payments. What does this protect? It protects the merchant from a payout they cannot check. It protects the bank's support team from arguing about a number nobody can take apart. And it protects both from the quietest mistake of all: a night that did not run, or a day counted twice. With a clear day, a rebuild that replaces, and every line behind every total, the accountant's question finally has an answer. Back to the till at closing time. One clear day, counted the same way every time, and every coin accounted for. Next: when a buyer says, I never bought this.
