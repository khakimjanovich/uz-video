Friday, nine at night, the bazaar is loud, and Aziz's bag is lighter than it was. His phone is gone, with the mobile bank and the marketplace still signed in. From his sister's phone he calls the bank and says one sentence: lock everything until I have a phone again. Who in this system is allowed to lock Aziz out of his own account? In lesson thirty-three, a refresh that gives a different personal identification number ended in a block. This lesson opens that block: who can set it, what it does at once, and who can lift it. The list of who can block is short, and I think its shortness is the design. Only two: the platform itself, when a refresh gives a different personal identification number, and an operator, with a reason. No other automatic block exists in this version. A number that the mobile operator gives to a new person never blocks its old owner. And a failed face check never blocks anybody. It only counts an attempt, for the phone number, the device and the personal identification number. After the first counted failure and after the second, a cool-down starts, and after the daily cap no attempt is possible until the next day. Only failures that the person or the capture caused are counted: a wrong document, a face that does not match, a poor image. A failure that the service caused, like a timeout, is not counted. Back to Aziz. The operator finds him in the panel and presses block, with the reason: phone stolen, customer request. Now watch what one block does. It ends every access grant of Aziz, in every app, on every device. It refuses every request from each of his devices, so the phone in the thief's pocket gets an error, not a balance. It refuses a registration or a login with his number. It stops each open proofing session. It keeps his devices and his identity data, because a block is a pause, not a deletion. It sends the security notification account blocked. It opens a case, or updates an open one. And it writes an audit event and sends an event to the bank's antifraud system. How can all of that be true at the very next request? The block makes three writes together: the customer state in Postgres, the sessions in Keycloak, and the outbox. The gateway also keeps a revocation list in Valkey, and every renewal passes the binding check first. So the stop is real at the next request, even at the busiest hour. The operator path and the automatic path make the same three writes, and only the actor is different. Now the case. A case is the operator's folder for one problem. It shows the customer, the trigger, the related values, masked, and the comparison notes. Cases also open for other reasons: data that the customer refused, a stopped re-registration, a link that was refused or expired. Phone reviews have their own list. Each case ends with one result: no action, unblocked, link set by the bank, or customer contacted. On Saturday, Aziz has a new phone with his old number. He calls again, and the operator unblocks him, with a reason. Only an operator can unblock. An unblock makes the devices trusted again and sends the notification account unblocked, with no new proofing. Read that once more: the devices. All of them, and that includes the phone the thief has. So the careful operator first revokes the stolen phone, and only then unblocks. The new phone then joins like any new device, with the checks of lesson twenty-four. There is one exception to the easy unblock. When the block came from a different personal identification number, an unblock does not give back any device. The customer must log in on a device and pass a full proofing, and only then does the platform record a trusted device again. That makes sense to me: after a mismatch, the platform does not know yet which face was the real one. To recap: two can block, the platform on a different number and an operator with a reason. A block ends every session at the next request, keeps the data, and opens a case. Only an operator unblocks, and the devices come back, except after a mismatch. But one thing from Friday night is still strange. Aziz got account blocked by SMS and by push, and one of his devices was in a thief's pocket. What can a message on a stolen phone show?
