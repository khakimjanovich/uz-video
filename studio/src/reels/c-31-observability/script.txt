Tuesday, twenty to nine in the evening. A buyer pays for roses on Malika's shop, and the payment page just spins. A minute later, Malika is on the phone to the bank. Dilnoza, in the bank's operations team, picks up. Malika says her customer's payment has been spinning for a whole minute. That payment crossed the payment page, ecom, the card zone, an adapter and the processing centre. Four machines touched it, and each one writes its own log lines. Without one number to follow, Dilnoza would search four logs and guess. And the lines that would help her most are the ones most likely to hold a card number. In lesson twenty-four, this path was built end to end, and in lesson three, card numbers on screens were cut to the first six and the last four digits. This lesson follows one payment across that whole path, and keeps every log line just as clean. Every request gets a trace: one number that follows it from the moment it arrives until its answer leaves. When ecom calls the card zone, the trace goes along. When the card zone calls an adapter, it goes along again. So one request draws one line across both products. Every answer names its trace too, even an answer that says no. Bekzod, who connected Malika's site, keeps that number with every order, so Malika reads it out over the phone. Support finds the whole story in seconds. Every log line carries the same number. So a warning in one log leads straight to the request that caused it, and to every other line about that request. Now the card numbers. I think the log is the leakiest place in any payment system. Most teams lock the database, and then print a card number into a log while chasing a bug. So here, before any line is written, it is cleaned. This is log masking. A card number keeps only its first six and its last four digits; the middle becomes stars. Any long run of digits that could be a card number is treated as one. A token keeps only its last four characters. Passwords and secrets are hidden completely. This happens on every line, wherever the line is sent. So nothing the running system writes can leak a card. Each product sends all of this to its own monitoring. ecom has its own, and the card zone runs its own, separately. They share nothing here either, just as on the map. Dilnoza opens ecom's monitoring. The first dashboard shows payments by how they end: paid, declined, and unknown. Unknown is painted red: payments where nobody yet knows whether money moved. Right now, the red line sits at zero. She pastes the trace number Malika read out. One line appears, crossing ecom, the card zone and the adapter, with the time each step took. The adapter waited almost a second for the processing centre. She opens the log lines of that request. The card number shows its first six digits, a row of stars, and its last four. Later that night, the processing centre goes quiet. Payments start to stay unknown, and the red line climbs. Dilnoza has gone home, and nobody is watching the screen. That does not matter, because of alert rules. An alert rule is a condition the system checks on its own, all the time, written down like the rest of the product. When payments stay unknown for too long, the rule fires. An email reaches Dilnoza, saying that payments are stuck. It says what is wrong and when it started. She opens the dashboard from the email and sees the same red line. Without the trace, Malika's simple question costs an hour of guessing. Without masking, the logs become a second place where card numbers live. Without alerts, a stuck payment is found by an angry buyer, not by the bank. Tonight, the bank knew first.
