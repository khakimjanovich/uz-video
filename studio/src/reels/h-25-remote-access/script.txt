Thursday, half past four. Aziz's phone rings, and a calm voice says that it is the security team of his bank. Someone is trying to take his money, the voice says, so he must install a small app, right now, to protect his account. The app lets another person see and control his screen. Aziz's phone is trusted, his session is valid, and his face is his own. So what can still go wrong? In lesson twenty-four, a new phone proved the SIM card, the old phone approved, and MyID proved the face. This lesson covers the one danger that none of those checks can see: someone who drives Aziz's own trusted phone. Look at the checks one by one. The phone is Trusted. The grant is valid. The binding check passes, because every request really comes from Aziz's device. Even the face is real, because Aziz himself holds the phone. From the gateway, everything looks perfect. The danger is not in the request. It is in the hands that make it. So the guard must live on the phone, not on the server. This is the job of the master app. The mobile bank looks for two things: remote-access software, and an active phone call. Only the master app does this. It moves money, so it carries the app self-protection of the bank. Side apps, like the marketplace, do not detect remote access at all. The caller's app is now installed, and it starts to share the screen. The mobile bank finds it. At once, the mobile bank blocks its own screen. The caller sees nothing useful, and he can tap nothing. Then the mobile bank tells the platform. It sends a remote-access event. The event carries three things: the device, the time, and the type. The type is either remote-access software, or a phone call. Now watch what the platform does, and what it does not do. It writes an audit event. It sends an event to the antifraud system of the bank. And that is all. The platform does not block Aziz. It does not change his customer record, his device or his grant. The phone stays trusted, and his session stays valid. At first this sounds too soft. A thief is on the line, so why not lock everything? I think the soft answer is the right one. A phone call is not a crime. Aziz calls his mother every evening. If every call blocked his account, honest people would be locked out every day, and they would stop trusting the bank. And a block would punish Aziz for what the caller did. So the work is split. The master app protects the screen at the moment of danger. The platform keeps an honest record. The antifraud system of the bank sees the pattern over time: the same caller, many customers, the same hour. In this first version, the antifraud system only receives events. The platform never waits for its answer. Back to Aziz. His screen is dark, and the caller is getting angry. That anger is a good sign. Aziz hangs up and calls the number on the back of his card. The real bank tells him that nobody from the bank asks for an app like that. This closes the module on devices and login. Lesson twenty gave the phone a record and a state. Lesson twenty-one gave it an access grant. Lesson twenty-two showed the way up to level two. Lessons twenty-three and twenty-four showed the two logins: on a known phone, and on a new one. And this lesson put a guard on the hands that hold the phone. To recap. Only the master app detects remote access. It blocks its own screen. The platform records the event and forwards it, and Aziz stays a customer. But the marketplace has none of this. It has no MyID, no TAF and no remote-access guard.
