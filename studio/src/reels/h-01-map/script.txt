Aziz uses three apps from his bank: the mobile bank, the marketplace and the credit service. Each app must know that it is really Aziz. But nobody wants a face check every time they open an app. So how can a bank know it is really Aziz, in every app, without checking his face every time? This course builds the answer: heeyID, a customer identity platform that the bank runs inside its own walls. Follow Aziz for one day, and the whole map appears. Aziz installs the mobile bank. It is the master app: it moves money, so only it may run MyID and TAF. He types his phone number, and an SMS code arrives. It comes through TAF, the state antifraud service of the mobile operators. TAF checks that the code arrived on this phone, with this SIM card, and its verdict is green. He types the code, then sets a password and types it a second time. This one password opens every app of the bank. Now Aziz has an account at level one. The bank knows his phone, but it does not yet know the person. In the marketplace, Aziz wants to buy a phone on credit. The credit service needs level two: it must know the real person, with a real passport. The marketplace is a side app, and it never checks a face, so it sends Aziz to the mobile bank. There, the app first asks for his consent, and records it. Then MyID, the national identity service, opens inside the mobile bank. It checks his face and his passport against the state records. The platform also checks his phone number against the numbers that MyID knows. Aziz is now at level two. Next, the platform looks for Aziz in the bank's own customer records, by his personal identification number. It finds him, and links the two. His phone becomes a trusted device. Back in the marketplace, Aziz taps Approve in the mobile bank, and the marketplace session gets level two too. Each app gets its own access grant: this is Aziz, at level two, for this app only. Next morning, Aziz opens the marketplace, then the mobile bank. No face check. No new code. His sessions simply continue on his trusted phone. One proofing serves every app of the bank. Now the worry. A stranger talks the mobile operator into moving Aziz's number to a new SIM card. He puts it in a new phone, installs the mobile bank and tries to log in as Aziz. He even guessed the password. What stops him? Not TAF alone: the SIM card really has Aziz's number, so the verdict can be green. But it is a new phone, and Aziz was at level two, so the mobile bank asks for MyID on this phone. The stranger's face is not Aziz's face. At the same time, Aziz's trusted phone shows the login request, with two buttons: Approve, and This was not me. Aziz presses This was not me, and the login stops. His number now goes on hold: no codes and no SMS to it, only push. So the marketplace web cannot help the stranger either: its login request also goes to Aziz's phone. Aziz changes his password at once. Then the app asks him: does your SIM card still work? It does not, so Aziz passes MyID again, the number leaves his account, and Aziz adds a new one. Now look at the map behind that day. The bank builds its own apps, and every app calls the heeyID API. Only the mobile bank holds the MyID and TAF SDKs. The other apps are side apps: they use the password, then an approval in the mobile bank or an SMS code. Every request from the apps enters through one edge, and goes to the customer gateway. All the rules of the bank live in that gateway: levels, limits, devices, sessions and consent. Behind the gateway is Keycloak. It does one job: it issues the tokens for the access grant. Each app is one client, and each token names its app, so a marketplace token never opens the mobile bank. The gateway never calls the outside world directly. It uses adapters: one each for TAF, MyID, the SMS service, the customer records and the push service. In the mobile bank, TAF sends the codes. The bank's SMS service sends them in the side apps, and in the mobile bank only when TAF is down. Behind the gateway are four stores. Postgres keeps the records. Valkey keeps the counters and the cool-downs. Kafka carries every event out, through an outbox. Vault keeps the keys. The bank's staff work in the administrator panel. It talks to the admin gateway, which uses the same rules, not a second copy. Now draw one frame around all of it: the bank's perimeter. One installation serves one bank, and a second bank gets its own installation. TAF reaches the platform through its own agent in the bank's network. Only MyID and the push service are outside, and the push service gets no personal data. To recap: Aziz proved who he is one time, and his trusted phone carries that proof to every app of the bank. So how many times does Aziz meet MyID? Once, and again only on a new phone, or when something risky happens. Next: the words of identity, from authentication to assurance level.
