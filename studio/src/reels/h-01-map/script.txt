Aziz sends money to his mother from his phone, and the bank asks for no face check. Yet the bank is sure that it is really Aziz. So how can a bank know it is really Aziz, without a face check every time? The answer is heeyID, a customer identity platform that the bank runs inside its own perimeter. This course follows Aziz on one journey, from his first code to his first transfer, and the map of the platform appears on the way. On a Sunday, Aziz installs the mobile bank. It is the master app of the bank: the app that moves money. He types his phone number, and an SMS code arrives. TAF sends it: the state antifraud service of the mobile operators. He types the code. TAF checks that the code arrived on this phone, with this SIM card, and its verdict is green. Then he sets a password and types it a second time. Now Aziz has an account at level one. The bank knows his phone number, but it does not yet know the person. The next Saturday, Aziz wants to send money to his mother. Money needs level two: the bank must know the real person, with a real passport. First, the app asks for his consent, and the platform records it. Then MyID, the national identity service, opens inside the mobile bank. It compares his live face and his passport with the state records. Next, the platform checks that the phone number is his. It looks first at the number on his MyID record. When MyID has no number, or a different one, TAF says who owns the number. Now Aziz is at level two. And his phone becomes the trusted phone: the one phone that moves his money. The transfer to his mother goes. The next day, Aziz opens the mobile bank again, and there is no face check. When he logs out, he logs in again on the same phone, with his password and a code. Now the worry. A stranger talks the mobile operator into a new SIM card with Aziz's number. He also has Aziz's password, from a leak on another website. He puts the SIM card in his own phone and installs the mobile bank. He has the number and the password, but he does not have the trusted phone. A phone becomes trusted only when Aziz's own face passes MyID on it, and TAF confirms its SIM card. The stranger cannot bring Aziz's face. So his phone never moves Aziz's money. Only the trusted phone moves money, and only the face of the real person makes a phone trusted. Now look at the map behind that journey. The mobile bank calls the heeyID API, and every request enters through one edge. The edge sends it to the customer gateway. All the rules of the bank live in that gateway: levels, limits, devices, sessions and consent. Next to the gateway is Keycloak, and it does one job: it issues the tokens that the app carries. The bank's staff work in the administrator panel. It talks to the admin gateway, which uses the same rules, not a second copy. Under the gateways are four stores. Postgres keeps the records. Valkey keeps the counters and the cool-downs. Kafka carries the events out. Vault keeps the keys. The gateway never calls the outside world directly. It uses adapters, one for each service: MyID, TAF, SMS and push. Only the customer gateway and the workers, which run the background jobs, call the adapters. Now draw one frame around all of it: the bank's perimeter. One installation serves one bank. The TAF agent runs inside, in the bank's network, and the TAF adapter calls it. Three services are outside: MyID, the SMS vendor and the push service. The push service gets no personal data. One person, one phone number, one password and one trusted phone: Aziz proves who he is one time, and his phone carries that proof every day. So how many times does Aziz meet MyID? Once. Lesson two gives the words behind this journey: authentication, identity proofing and authorisation.
