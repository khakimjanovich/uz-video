Sunday, a quarter past nine in the morning. At a bus stop, a buyer orders tulips from Malika's shop, and presses pay. Malika's site does not ask for the card. It sends the buyer to a link. In lesson twenty-five, ecom answered Bekzod's create request with that link, and in lesson five, the bank's page held the card frame from the card zone. This lesson is that page, built so that only this buyer can open it. Malika must never touch a card number, so the card cannot be typed on her site. And the page cannot sit at an address anyone could guess by changing a number. It must open only for the person who got the link. It must work only for a while. It must show exactly what is being paid. And it must survive a buyer with cold fingers who presses Confirm twice. When Bekzod's server creates the payment, the bank's answer carries the link. The link holds a page token: a long, random, one-time key. The bank does not keep the token itself. It keeps only a fingerprint of it, so even someone reading the bank's records cannot rebuild the link. And the link expires after the bank's chosen time. The buyer taps it, and the page loads. It shows Malika's website name and logo, the order, the amount, and the cards this website accepts. Those are the cards with a live terminal behind them. Then the page opens a card entry session with the card zone, and puts the card zone's frame inside the page. The card number goes into that frame, and only into that frame. A payment gets only the bank's chosen number of these sessions. After that, the payment fails, and no money moves. When the buyer finishes typing, the frame sends the page one small message. The page accepts it only from the frame's own address, and only if it holds a token. It never accepts a card number. The page passes the token to ecom. ecom asks the card zone whether this card belongs to this payment's session. Only then does the charge begin. And a page lock lets only one step run at a time for each payment. Here is the page on the buyer's phone. At the top, a line says secure payment page of the bank. Below it are the website, the order number, the amount to pay, the accepted cards, and a line that says valid until, with the time. Under that sits the card details frame. The buyer double clicks Confirm. The first click goes through. The second is turned away, because the page is busy. Only one step runs. Now suppose the bus arrives, and the frame stays open too long, or the buyer makes too many tries. The page says that attempt ended, and asks to enter the card again. If every attempt is used up, the page says it closed before any money moved, and asks for a new link from the shop. A buyer who opens the link again next week sees that it has expired. And when the payment ends, the page shows a link back to the shop. Every one of these rules closes a door. Without the page token, anyone could walk through payment pages by guessing addresses. Without the fingerprint, a leaked copy of the bank's records would hand out live links. Without the frame, the card number would pass through ecom and Malika's shop. Without the lock, one double click could start two charges. My advice is simple. If your page address holds a number you can count up, you have built a page anyone can open. Here, the buyer sees exactly what they pay, and Malika never touches a card. Next: the charge itself.
