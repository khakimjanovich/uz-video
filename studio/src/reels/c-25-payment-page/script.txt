How does a buyer reach the bank's payment page, and nobody else? The merchant has created a payment. Now the buyer has to pay it. The buyer must not type a card on the shop's own site, because the shop must never touch a card number. And the page cannot sit at an address anyone could guess by changing a number. It must open only for the person who got the link. It must work only for a while. It must show exactly what is being paid. And it must survive a buyer who presses Pay twice. Think of a hotel key card. It opens one room. It works for the guest who holds it. And it stops working at checkout. The bank's payment page works the same way. When the merchant creates a payment, the bank answers with a link. The link carries a page token: a long, random, one-time key. The merchant sends the buyer to that link. The bank does not keep the token itself. It keeps only a fingerprint of it, so even someone reading the bank's records cannot rebuild the link. And the link expires after the bank's chosen time. When the buyer opens the link, the bank's payment page loads. It shows the website's name and logo, the order, the amount, and the cards this website accepts. Those are the cards with a live terminal behind them. Then the page opens a card-entry session with the card zone, and puts the card zone's frame inside the page. The card number goes into that frame, and only into that frame. A payment gets only the bank's chosen number of these sessions. After that, the payment fails, and no money moves. When the buyer finishes typing, the frame sends the page one small message. The page accepts it only from the frame's own address, and only if it holds a token. It never accepts a card number. The page passes the token to ecom. ecom asks the card zone whether this card belongs to this payment's session. Only then does the charge begin. And a page lock lets only one step run at a time for each payment. Now watch the product from the buyer's side. The buyer taps the link in the shop's checkout. The page opens with a line at the top: secure payment page of the bank. Below it are the website, the order number, the amount to pay, the accepted cards, and a line that says valid until, with the time. Under that sits the card details frame. The buyer double-clicks the pay button. The first click goes through. The second is turned away, because the page is busy. Only one step runs. Suppose the buyer leaves the frame open too long, or makes too many tries. The page says that attempt ended, and asks to enter the card again. If the buyer uses up every attempt, the page says the payment page closed before any money moved, and asks for a new link from the shop. And a buyer who opens an old link sees that the link has expired. When the payment ends, the page shows a back to the shop link. What does this protect? Without the page token, anyone could walk through payment pages by guessing addresses. Without the fingerprint, a leaked copy of the bank's records would hand out live links. Without the frame, the card number would pass through ecom and the shop. Without the lock, one double click could start two charges. The page protects the buyer, who sees exactly what is being paid. It protects the shop, which never touches a card. So back to the key card. One guest, one room, until checkout. Next: the charge itself.
