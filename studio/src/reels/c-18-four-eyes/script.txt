Monday, four in the afternoon. Malika calls the bank: her papers are verified and her contract is signed, so can her shop start tonight? Dilnoza has the shop open on her screen, and she cannot switch it on. Not alone. In lesson seventeen, Dilnoza verified every document and signed the contract, and this lesson is about the last step, the one that lets a shop take real money. One admin, rushed or bribed, could switch on a shop nobody else looked at. By the time anyone noticed, it would have taken a week of payments with stolen cards. So there is no button that lets one admin switch a merchant on. Under Actions, Dilnoza sees "Request activation", with a short line: ask a second admin to switch the merchant on. She clicks it. The page says "Activation requested. Another admin has to approve it in Approvals." Dilnoza is now the maker: the person who asks. Nothing changes yet. Malika's shop stays exactly where it was. Out of habit, Dilnoza opens Approvals. Her request is there, with who requested it and when it expires. But the Approve and Reject buttons are grey. Hovering over them shows the message: "You made this request. Another admin has to decide it." The maker can never approve their own request. The screen does not offer it, and the server refuses it anyway, whatever the screen does. Ten minutes later, a colleague signs in and opens Approvals. For this second admin, the buttons are live. The colleague is the checker: the person who agrees, or refuses. One click on Approve. Only now does the merchant become active, and the page says the company is now active. At that moment the documents are checked again, in case something changed meanwhile. Had nobody decided in time, the request would have expired. So a forgotten request cannot be approved months later, when nobody remembers why it was opened. On the merchant's page, the history shows a new line. It names the checker as the one who made the move, and the reason says Dilnoza asked. This is four eyes: two people look at every change that matters. It is not a rule for merchants only. Along the top of Approvals wait the other kinds: fee changes, refund windows, and refunds that bank staff start. Anything that moves money or switches something on needs two people. One person proposes; another person decides. Every one of them uses the same screen, the same grey buttons for the maker, and the same history. Teams often call this red tape. I think it is the opposite. With one person, a single mistake or a single bribe is enough to let a bad shop take money. With two, it is not. And nobody can pressure Dilnoza into acting alone, because acting alone is not possible, and the history shows both names. At half past four, Dilnoza calls Malika back: your company is active.
