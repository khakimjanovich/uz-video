Why should one person never switch a merchant on alone? In lesson seventeen, a bank admin checked every document, and the merchant was verified. The last step, switching the merchant on, lets it take real money. This lesson makes that step need two different people. Picture one bank admin, rushed or bribed, who switches on a shop nobody else looked at. By the time anyone notices, that shop has taken a week of payments with stolen cards. Some company cheques need two signatures, so one person alone cannot spend the money. Switching a merchant on works the same way. This is four eyes: two people look at every change that matters. Here is how it works. There is no button that lets one admin switch a merchant on. Instead, one admin, the maker, opens a request. The maker is the person who asks. Nothing changes yet. The merchant stays exactly where it was. A different admin, the checker, decides the request. The checker is the person who agrees, or refuses. Only when the checker approves does the merchant become active. The maker can never approve their own request. The screen does not offer it, and the server refuses it anyway, whatever the screen does. A request that nobody decides in time expires. So a forgotten request cannot be approved months later, when nobody remembers why it was opened. On approval, the documents are checked again, in case something changed meanwhile. And the history names both people: the checker who approved it, and the maker who asked. This is not a rule for merchants only. It is how the whole product works. Anything that moves money or switches something on needs two people. A new fee rule needs a second admin. So does a longer refund window, and so does a refund that bank staff start. One person proposes; another person decides. Every one of them uses the same screen, the same grey buttons for the maker, and the same history. Now watch the product. The viewer is the first admin, on the approved merchant's page. Under Actions there is "Request activation", with a short line: ask a second admin to switch the merchant on. One click. The page says "Activation requested. Another admin has to approve it in Approvals." Now the same admin opens Approvals. The request is there, with who requested it and when it expires. But the Approve and Reject buttons are grey. Hovering over them shows the message: "You made this request. Another admin has to decide it." The screen will not let the maker decide. Next, a second admin signs in and opens Approvals. The same request appears, and for this person the buttons are live. They click Approve. The page says the company is now active. Back on the merchant's page, the history shows a new line. It names the second admin as the one who made the move, and the reason says who requested it. Along the top of Approvals, the viewer sees the other kinds of request waiting for a second person: fee changes, refund windows and refunds. What does this protect? With one person, a single mistake or a single bribe is enough to let a bad shop take money. With two, it is not. Four eyes protects buyers from shops nobody checked. It protects the bank from one person's error. And it protects the admins themselves, because nobody can be pressured into acting alone, and the history shows both names. So, like a cheque with two signatures: two people, every time money moves or something switches on. Next: how a merchant's server logs in.
