Can a payments product be secure on its own? Lesson four put card numbers in one locked room, and lesson forty shipped that room as fixed releases. This lesson asks the last question: who answers for the building around it. Picture a bank that buys a product. Its brochure says: compliant. Then the bank's assessor arrives. An assessor is the independent expert who checks a bank against the card industry's security standard, PCI DSS. The assessor asks for the firewall rules, the records of who logged in, the people who hold the keys, and the cameras on the server room. The vendor has none of them. Those belong to the bank's whole building, not to one room inside it. Worse, nobody wrote down which jobs were the product's and which were the bank's, so some fell between. Here is the picture this course ends on. We deliver the locked room. The bank owns the building around it: its walls, its guards, its cameras and its doors. So the room must fit into the building without breaking it. It has exactly two narrow doors: one for payers typing a card, and one for the bank's own systems. Nothing else on its server listens. Its database is never reachable from outside. It shares no part with ecom or with anything else: its own database, its own keys, its own logins. The bank's network wraps around it, and the bank builds the firewalls. Each product ships with a guide. It says three things: what the building must provide, how to install the room, and who does what. Who does what is the responsibility matrix. For each of the standard's twelve requirements, it says what the product does, what is the bank's, and what is shared. A product row describes what the product does; it is not a claim of compliance. Take the network: the product opens only two doors, the bank builds the firewalls, and the list of allowed connections is shared. Take the logs: the product keeps the trails, and collecting and reviewing them every day is the bank's. ecom has its own matrix. It holds no card number, so it gets the smaller check from Lesson four, and exactly which requirements apply is the assessor's call. Now the process that keeps it right. Every release is checked against the standard's requirements, one by one. Each is marked as met by the product, partly met, the bank's, shared, or a gap. Every gap goes on a list: the known gaps. Each known gap names the requirement it touches, and what the bank does until the product fixes it. For example, after every restart three keyholders must come to unseal Vault, so the bank keeps three of them within reach. And anything that moves money or keys takes two people. Switching a merchant on, changing a fee, approving a dispute loss, writing off a difference: one person asks, and another decides. Opening the keys takes three of five keyholders, and the bank makes sure no one holds two shares. Let's watch the product. The bank's install team opens the card zone's guide at the who-does-what table. Twelve rows, three columns: product, the bank, shared. They follow the network row across, then the logging row. Then they turn to the known gaps. Each line reads: the gap, the requirement it touches, and what the bank does until it is fixed. Finally, the assessor walks the whole building, the guide in hand. What does this protect? It protects the bank from a promise no product can keep on its own. It protects the room, because every job outside it has a named owner. And it protects honesty: each gap is written down with what covers it, never hidden. The product is not certified on its own. The assessor judges the whole building, with the guide as the product's evidence, and the guide ships with every release. Back to the map from Lesson one: the shop, the locked room, and the building around them both. We deliver the locked room; the bank owns the building. That is the end of phase one: thank you for walking the whole product, from the Pay button to the building around it.
