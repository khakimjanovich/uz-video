Aziz orders a bookshelf in the marketplace app, and it must come to his flat. He taps Add address. The platform already knows his registered address, because MyID gave it at his proofing. The marketplace is an app of the same bank, on the same phone, for the same person. So why should anyone ask Aziz before the marketplace gets that address? In lesson twenty-seven, Aziz linked his marketplace account in the mobile bank. This lesson is the first time that the marketplace wants something from his verified identity. Start with what the marketplace can normally see. Each app has its own sessions, and its backend accepts only its own tokens. Lesson five showed that wall. Through it, the marketplace gets a short summary of the customer: the level, the verified phone number, and a display name. The summary never holds an address. The address sits in the protected part of the record, with the passport data. The bank's keys encrypt it, and every read of it is audited. So there is exactly one way for an address to leave: a sharing request that Aziz approves. Here is how it runs. The marketplace backend asks the platform for a sharing request, with its own marketplace token. The platform first checks two things. Is Aziz at level two? And is his marketplace account linked? If either answer is no, the platform does not ask him. It tells the marketplace to send Aziz to the mobile bank first. Aziz is at level two, and his account is linked, so the request goes on. The platform keeps it for the configured waiting time, and sends a push signal to the mobile bank. Again, the push carries only the request identifier. The mobile bank pulls the request, and asks one question. Share your verified address with the marketplace? Aziz taps Share. Now the platform reads the address from the protected record, for one purpose: address sharing. It writes an audit event that names the reader, the marketplace, and the purpose. The marketplace collects the address one time, and saves it. The bookshelf has a place to go. Now the other answers. If Aziz taps Refuse, nothing is saved, and the marketplace gets a refusal. If he does not answer in the configured time, the result is the same: nothing is saved. The audit trail records the refusal too. Next month, Aziz adds his address once more, this time on the marketplace website. The mobile bank asks again. There is no standing consent: one answer covers one address, one time. I think this is the right choice, even if it costs one more tap. A box that Aziz ticked once, long ago, would let the marketplace read his address forever, after every move, without him ever seeing it again. A fresh question each time keeps the decision with Aziz, at the moment it matters. And when he moves out of that flat? Aziz deletes the saved address in the marketplace itself. The platform does not take part in that deletion. Once shared, the copy belongs to the marketplace, and the marketplace must let him remove it. Look at what this design does not do. The marketplace never holds a key to the identity record. It never reads the passport data. It cannot ask for the address on a session at level one. And it cannot skip the question, because the question lives in the mobile bank, not in the marketplace. To recap: the address leaves the protected record only through a sharing request. The request needs level two and a linked account. The mobile bank asks each time, the platform audits each read, and the customer deletes saved addresses in the marketplace. Aziz now has many sessions: the mobile bank on his phone, the marketplace app, and a browser at his parents' house. Suppose a stranger gets a session of his own in some other browser, and tries to end Aziz's session in the mobile bank. Which session wins? Next: sessions and their rank.
