Malika has a question for the bank, and it is a fair one: why does she pay more per bouquet than the shop next door? Dilnoza can answer it in one minute, and this lesson is why. In lesson two, the acquirer kept its fee and paid Malika the rest, and in lesson twenty-one, each website got its own deal, and this lesson decides that fee, for every website and every card. The easy way is to write the fee straight into the payment code, one rate for everyone. Then one merchant agrees a lower rate, one website gets a special offer, and one card costs the bank more. Soon the code is a tangle nobody dares touch. So the bank uses fee rules. A fee rule is one price the bank sets. It can apply to one website, one merchant, one card, or the whole bank. When a payment arrives, the most specific rule wins. A website's own rule beats its merchant's rule. A merchant's rule beats a card's rule. And the bank's default is used only when nothing more specific applies. The shop next door has its own merchant rule, and Malika pays the bank's default. The fee itself is a percent of the amount, plus a fixed part. It is kept between a minimum and a maximum. Money is always counted in whole cents, never in fractions of a cent. So the percent is rounded once, to whole cents, and the sum is exact. Otherwise small fractions would leak away across millions of payments. Dilnoza opens Fees. The page says it plainly: the most specific rule wins, and a new rule applies after a second admin approves it. Filters show the rules in force now, the ones waiting for approval, or all of them. This month the bank's default is changing, so she clicks New rule. Under "Applies to" there are three choices: merchant, website and card. A hint says to leave everything empty for the bank default. If she picked a merchant but no card, the form would say a merchant or website rule needs a card. She leaves the scope empty, sets a percent, a fixed part and a minimum, and clicks Send for approval. The page says another admin has to approve it. Here is the rule I would defend hardest: rules are never edited in place. A change is a new rule, proposed by one admin and approved by a second, the four eyes from lesson eighteen. In Approvals, under Fee changes, Dilnoza's own buttons are grey. A colleague signs in, opens the same tab, and clicks Approve. On Fees, the rule now shows as in force, with who proposed it and who approved it. Two rules for the same thing can never apply at the same moment: the database itself refuses an overlap. Now Malika's question. Dilnoza opens the Fee calculator. Its hint says it shows what a payment would pay right now. She chooses Malika's shop and her website, because a quote needs both. The amount is already filled in. She clicks Calculate. The answer shows the fee, the rule used, and how much Malika receives. And every payment keeps the rule it used, its fee, and what the merchant receives. So if Malika asks next year about last March, the answer is still there, whatever the rules say today. Next: the virtual card machine each payment goes through.
