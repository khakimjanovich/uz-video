Who decides the bank's fee on a payment? Picture the fee written straight into the payment code, one rate for everyone. Then one merchant agrees a lower rate. One website gets a special offer. One card costs the bank more. Soon the code is a tangle nobody dares touch. Worse, someone changes a rate in place, and last month's payments can no longer be explained. In a supermarket, the tag on the item beats the sign on the aisle, which beats the offer for the whole store. The most specific price wins. The bank's fee works the same way. Here is how it works. A fee rule is one price the bank sets. It can apply to one website, one merchant, one card, or the whole bank. When a payment arrives, the most specific rule wins. A website's own rule beats its merchant's rule. A merchant's rule beats a card's rule. And the bank's default is used only when nothing more specific applies. The fee itself is a percent of the amount, plus a fixed part. It is kept between a minimum and a maximum. Money is always counted in whole cents, never in fractions of a cent. So the percent is rounded once, to whole cents, and the sum is exact. Rules are never edited in place. A change is proposed by one admin, and a second admin approves it. Two rules for the same thing can never apply at the same moment: the database itself refuses an overlap. And every payment keeps the rule it used, its fee, and what the merchant receives. So last month's payments can always be explained, whatever the rules say today. Now watch the product. The viewer signs in as a bank admin and opens Fees. The page says it plainly: the most specific rule wins, and a new rule applies after a second admin approves it. Filters show the rules in force now, the ones waiting for approval, or all of them. The viewer clicks New rule. Under "Applies to" there are three choices: merchant, website and card. A hint says to leave everything empty for the bank default. If the viewer picks a merchant but no card, the form says a merchant or website rule needs a card. The viewer leaves the scope empty, sets a percent, a fixed part and a minimum, and clicks Send for approval. The page says another admin has to approve it. In Approvals, under Fee changes, the maker's own buttons are grey. A second admin signs in, opens the same tab, and clicks Approve. Back on Fees, the rule now shows as in force, with who proposed it and who approved it. Now the Fee calculator. Its hint says it shows what a payment would pay right now. The viewer chooses a merchant and a website, because a quote needs both. The amount is already filled in. They click Calculate. The answer shows the fee, the rule used, and how much the merchant receives. What does this protect? Without rules, every new deal is a change to the code. Without four eyes, one admin could quietly give a friend a lower rate. Without whole cents, small fractions would leak away across millions of payments. And without the rule kept on each payment, nobody could answer a merchant who asks why they paid what they paid. Fee rules protect merchants, the bank and its auditors. So, like price tags: the most specific wins, and nobody repaints the old ones. Next: the virtual card machine each payment goes through.
