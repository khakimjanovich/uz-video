Who decides the bank's fee on a payment? Picture a fee that starts as one number in the payment code: one and a half percent. Then one merchant agrees a lower rate, one website gets a special offer, and one card costs the bank more. The number turns into a tangle of if statements that nobody dares to touch. Worse, someone edits a rate in place, and last month's payments can no longer be explained. A supermarket solves this with price tags. The tag on the item beats the sign on the aisle, which beats the offer for the whole store. The most specific price wins. Here, each price is a fee rule. A fee rule can apply to one website, one merchant, one card, or the whole bank. When several match a payment, the most specific one wins. The fee is a percent of the amount, plus a fixed part, kept between a minimum and a maximum. Money is counted in minor units, like cents, never in decimals. So the percent is rounded once, to whole minor units. Rules are never edited in place. A change is proposed, and a second admin approves it, as lesson eighteen showed. Two rules for the same thing can never apply at the same moment: the database refuses it. And each payment keeps the rule it used, its fee, and the rest the merchant receives. So last month's payments can always be explained. Now read the price in the code. The fee is the percent, rounded half up to whole minor units, plus the fixed part, then kept between the minimum and the maximum. And the most specific rule scores highest: a website four, a merchant two, a card one. Now try the formula yourself, in J shell. Take an amount in cents, and a rule of one and a half percent, plus a fixed five hundred. The percent comes to eighteen thousand five hundred and eighteen and a half, which rounds up to eighteen thousand five hundred and nineteen. Add the fixed part, and the fee is nineteen thousand and nineteen cents. The merchant receives the rest. Like price tags: the most specific wins, and nobody repaints the old ones.
