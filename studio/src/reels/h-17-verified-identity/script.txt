The lift doors open, and Aziz taps Confirm under his own data. His names, his date of birth, and parts of his document number and his personal identification number. A minute ago, MyID read all of it from state records, and now it sits in a database of the bank. Who can read it there? In the lift, MyID checked his face, and the platform exchanged the one-time code for the result. The platform now keeps that result as his verified identity. Three things guard it: what the record holds, who may open it, and the trace that each read leaves. First, what it holds. The verified identity is a protected class of data, with stricter rules than any other record. It holds his names, his date and place of birth, his personal identification number, gender, citizenship and nationality, his document data and his registered addresses. It also holds the phone number that MyID gave, and an identifier from MyID with its expiry date. Images from the camera stay only when the bank turns image storage on. Nothing else lives in this class. His phone number, his phone and his network address are personal data too, but other rules handle them. The record is encrypted, and only the Vault of the bank can open it, as lesson six showed. No part of the system keeps a clear copy longer than one request, and the identity never goes to a log. Now, who may open it. Inside the gateway, only one closed part of the code may touch this record. Every other part must ask it, and name a purpose. By default, the answer has masked values: only a few characters of each value show. Full values leave only for a short list of purposes. Keeping a new MyID result. The confirmation screen that Aziz just saw, and only on the phone of the session that gave the result. The phone check, which compares his number with the number from MyID. And the deletion job, when the retention time ends. An operator who opens a case or a phone review sees masked values only. Now the other side: who never gets it. The mobile bank gets only an identity summary: his level and his display name. No personal identification number, no document number, no address and no image. The bank's antifraud system, the notifications and the audit trail never get it either. Last, the trace. Every release is an audit event, with the reader and the purpose. Suppose an operator opens a case about Aziz. She sees the first two and last two digits of his personal identification number, and the last four characters of his document. The audit trail records her, the purpose and the time. Encryption stops a person who steals a copy of the disk. The audit trail stops a person who already has access, because each read carries a name. So the bank can always tell Aziz who read his data, when, and why. Now a check for you. The mobile bank shows Aziz his name on the home screen: does the app also get his personal identification number? No: the app gets only the identity summary, his level and his display name, and the full number stays behind the one closed door. One protected class holds his verified identity, and nothing else. The keys of the bank lock it, and only one part of the gateway opens it. Reads are masked by default, full values go only to a short list of purposes, and every release leaves an audit event. One field in the record still waits for a check: the phone number from MyID. On his record, it is his old university number, not the number in his phone.
