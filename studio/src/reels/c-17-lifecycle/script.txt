How does a new merchant earn the right to take card payments? In lesson sixteen, a checked email created the merchant, and made its first admin. But that merchant cannot take any money yet. This lesson shows how it earns that right, one checked document at a time. Picture a company that signs up on Monday and takes payments on Tuesday. Nobody saw its founding papers, or its director's passport. When complaints and disputed payments pile up, the bank cannot say who it signed with, or who let it in. To get a driving licence, you hand in your papers, and each one is checked. You get a licence with its own number, and every step goes in your file. A merchant earns its states the same way. Here is how it works. First, the merchant uploads its company documents. The bank must know who it does business with. This is know your business. Each file goes into the file store. The store reads each file's real type from the file's own contents, never from its name. So a file called "charter" that is really something else is refused. The store also keeps a checksum: a fingerprint worked out from every byte of the file. When the file is read back, its fingerprint must still match, so a changed file is caught. Next, a bank admin checks every document. Each one is verified, or rejected with a reason the merchant can read and fix. Only when every required document is verified can the company itself be verified. Then it signs a contract, and the contract carries its own unique number. No two merchants ever share one. Every move between states is written down: from where, to where, who, and why. That record is the status history, and it is written in the same step as the move itself. A move without its record cannot happen. Now watch the product. The viewer signs in as the merchant and opens Documents. The page says "Company documents", and explains that the bank checks who it works with before switching a merchant on. Two papers are marked Required: the company charter and the director's passport. Two more say "If it applies": a licence and a power of attorney. The viewer clicks Choose file, picks a sample charter, and clicks Upload. The page says "Uploaded. The bank will review it." The status reads "Waiting for review". Now the trick. The viewer uploads a file whose name says it is a document, but whose contents are something else. The picker lets it through, because it only looks at the name. The server reads the contents and refuses it. Next, the bank admin portal. The admin opens Merchants and picks this merchant. Each document has two buttons, Verify and Reject. Reject asks the admin to say what is wrong, so the merchant can fix it. The admin verifies both. If the merchant had not yet confirmed its email, the "Approve documents" button would stay grey, with a message saying so. Here the email is confirmed, so the admin clicks Approve documents. Then Sign contract, and types the contract number, as printed on the signed paper. Last, the admin opens History. Every move is there: from, to, who, and the reason. What does this protect? Without checked papers, the bank cannot say who it is paying. Without a fingerprint, a document could be swapped after it was checked. Without the history, nobody could say who approved what. Know your business protects the bank, the card network and every buyer. The status history protects the people who did the checking, because it shows exactly what they saw and when. So, like a driving licence: checked papers, a unique number, and every step in the file. Next: why switching a merchant on needs two people.
