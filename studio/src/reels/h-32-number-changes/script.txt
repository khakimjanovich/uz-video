After the SIM swap, Aziz buys a new SIM card. The operator gives him a number that someone else used for years. That someone is still a customer of the bank, and the number still sits on their account. He puts it into a new phone, and types the number into the mobile bank. To the platform, Aziz now looks exactly like a thief: a known number, a strange phone, and no password. How does the platform tell a new owner of a number from a thief of one? In lesson thirty-one, the number left Aziz's account after the SIM question. This lesson covers three ways that a number and a person drift apart: number reassignment, re-registration, and a dormant account. Start with the reassignment. The number belongs to an active customer, so this is not a registration. The mobile bank asks for that customer's password, and Aziz does not know it. So he taps Forgot password, and the reset runs the checks of a new phone. TAF sends a code, and it lands on Aziz's SIM card, because the number really is his now. The old owner was at level two, so the account check asks for MyID. MyID checks the face, and the face is Aziz's. The personal identification number from MyID belongs to a different person than the old owner. That is the signal. A thief fails the face. A new owner passes it, and turns out to be someone else. So the platform removes the number from the old owner. It does not block the old owner. They did nothing wrong: the operator simply gave their old number away. Their sessions end. A push message, number removed, goes to all their devices, and they verify a new number at their next login. The audit trail records the event number reassigned, and the antifraud system hears about it. I think this is the most important line in the lesson: never a block. A number changing hands is normal life, not fraud. Now, who gets the number? The platform looks at the person that MyID found. If that person has no account, and MyID lists this number for them, they become a new customer at level two. If MyID does not list it, they start at level one, with the number under a phone review. But Aziz is already a customer, with his old number. So the second rule applies: re-registration. Re-registration is for one person who arrives under a second number. The usual shape is simple. Someone registers a new number, at level one, and later steps up with MyID. MyID says: this person already exists, with a different number. Now the platform asks one question. Is the new number on MyID's list of numbers for that person? If yes, the platform moves the number to the existing customer. The new phone becomes the trusted device. Every earlier device of the customer is revoked, with the reason replaced by re-registration. The short level-one record is closed. And an SMS goes to the old number: a new phone was registered. Aziz's new number is on his MyID list, so this is his path. His new phone is now trusted, and his old phone is revoked. If the number is not on the list, the platform does nothing on its own. The step-up stops, the level-one customer stays as it was, and a case opens for an operator. Re-registrations are also limited per day, so nobody can move numbers around in a loop. The third case is about time. Suppose a customer does not touch the bank for a long while. None of the devices are used for longer than the configured dormancy period. That account is dormant. A dormant account never opens from a new phone with a one-time code alone. Old numbers get new owners, and nobody is watching the old sessions. So the platform asks for more. A customer who was ever at level two passes a MyID re-check, or a full proofing when the reusable identifier has expired. In a side app, the login starts at level one, and level two needs the approval in the master app. A dormant customer who was never at level two has no face on file. So the platform locks the old record, and makes a new customer at level one. The audit trail records a dormant account reset. Nothing is handed over on a code alone. And when MyID finds a different person here too, it is a number reassignment again. Each of these cases runs in one database transaction, together with its events. So a crash can never leave one number on two customers. To recap: a number belongs to a person only while MyID agrees. A new owner proves the face, and the number moves, with no block on the old owner. One person with two numbers is joined by re-registration, but only when MyID lists the number. And a dormant account needs more than a code. Aziz is settled again: one number, one trusted phone, level two. But his passport, the one MyID checked, expires next year.
