What does a fee change have to do with card numbers? Picture your first ticket on a payments team: add a fee to card payments. You open the code and find card numbers, fees and merchants in one big service. So a simple fee change touches the card code, and it waits for a card security review. This system splits that service in two. Each part is a unit: a separate product with its own code, its own install and its own data. The big unit is ecom, the shop. The small unit is the cards unit, a locked room next door. Three kinds of people use the system. The merchant's server asks for payments. The buyer pays on the payment page. And the bank's staff look after the merchants. ecom is one app built in five parts, and each part is a module. The modules hold the merchants, the fees, the terminals, the payments, and the checking of the books. The cards unit is the only place a card number lives. It is where the buyer types the card, where the number is kept, and where the lines go out to the card networks. Each unit has its own database, its own keys and its own logins. They share nothing, so nothing in the shop can open the locked room. One line joins them. ecom calls the cards unit, and the cards unit never calls ecom. So the cards unit is installed first, and ecom second. Now back to the ticket. The fee change lives in the shop alone, in one module, and it never goes near the locked room. This course builds the locked room first, then the shop. Now get both units running on your laptop. Clone the seven repositories into one folder. Run the cards installer first, then the ecom installer. Each installer gets its own lesson near the end of the course. Then ask Docker Compose what each unit runs. The cards unit lists its own Postgres, its own Keycloak and its card services. ecom lists its own Postgres, its own Keycloak and its own app. Two lists, two copies of everything, nothing shared.
