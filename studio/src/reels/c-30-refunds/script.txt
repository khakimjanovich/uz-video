How does a merchant give money back? In lesson two, money went back by reversal before the day closed, and by refund after. In lesson twenty-seven, one caller won the charge, and the logbook was written before the card zone was asked. This lesson gives money back the same careful way. A buyer returns a pair of shoes. Now picture three things going wrong. The merchant presses refund twice. The bank's support team refunds the same payment again over the phone. And a refund made six months later reaches a card that no longer exists. Each mistake pays out too much, or too late. Go back to the tab book from the second lesson. Before the day closes, the shopkeeper simply crosses the line out. That is a reversal, and it costs nothing. After the day closes, the shopkeeper hands the money back. That is a refund. The day decides which one happens. The day is counted in the bank's own time zone. And the whole amount always goes back, never a part. The merchant can give back a paid payment by itself, but only up to its refund cap. That is the bank's chosen largest amount a merchant may give back on its own. A later refund must also fall inside the refund window. That is the bank's chosen number of working days after the payment. Two bank admins together can make one website's window longer. Inside these limits, giving back works like the charge. One claim lets only one caller win, however many clicks arrive. A line is written in the bank's logbook first, with the merchant's reason. Then ecom asks the card zone, by token, with the call's own id. Approved is final. Refused leaves the payment paid, and the merchant is told the refund failed. No answer is asked about later, the same way as a silent charge, and never sent twice. Beyond the cap, or after the window, the merchant cannot give money back alone. The bank does it instead. That is a refund by application. The merchant writes to the bank. One bank admin asks for the refund, and a second admin approves it. Now watch the product. In the merchant portal, open a paid payment. A box says refundable until, with the date, and how many days are left. Press give back. A window asks: give the money back? It explains that the whole amount goes back to the buyer's card, reversed today, or refunded later. It asks for a reason, which both the merchant and the bank will see. Confirm it. The status turns to giving the money back. Moments later it says reversed, because it is still the same day. Under calls to the card scheme, a reversal line appears below the debit. On the buyer's page, it says money returned. Now open a large payment, above the cap. There is no give back button. A grey note says above the refund limit, and that larger refunds are made by the bank on a written application. Open an old payment. The note says the refund window has closed, and gives the date it could be refunded until. A teammate who only views sees no refund box at all. Now switch to the bank's admin portal. An admin opens that old payment and chooses refund by application. They enter the application number and a note saying why. They press send for approval, and the screen says it was sent to another admin. Only when a second admin approves does the money go back. What does this protect? The claim stops double refunds. The logbook means every refund can be explained. The cap and the window protect the merchant from large or late mistakes. And two admins protect the bank from one person moving money alone. So back to the tab book. Cross it out today, or hand it back later. Once, written down first, and within the shop's rules. Next: following one payment through the whole system.
