Tuesday, eleven in the morning. A bouquet ordered and paid at nine arrives wilted, and the buyer asks for the money back. Malika is happy to give it, as long as nothing goes wrong on the way. In lesson two, a payment cancelled the same day was reversed and a settled one was refunded, and in lesson twenty-seven, one caller won the charge and the logbook was written before the card zone was asked. This lesson gives money back the same careful way. Here is what goes wrong without care. Malika presses give back twice. The bank's support team refunds the same payment again over the phone. A refund made six months later reaches a card that no longer exists. Each mistake pays out too much, or too late. The first rule depends on the day. Before the day closes, the payment is simply reversed, and it costs nothing. After the day closes, the money travels back as a refund. The day is counted in the bank's own time zone. And the whole amount always goes back, never a part. Malika can give back a paid payment by herself, but only up to the refund cap. That is the bank's chosen largest amount a merchant may give back on its own. A later refund must also fall inside the refund window. That is the bank's chosen number of working days after the payment. Two bank admins together can make one website's window longer. Inside these limits, giving back works like the charge. One claim lets only one caller win, however many clicks arrive. A line is written in the bank's logbook first, with the merchant's reason. Then ecom asks the card zone, by token, with the call's own id. Approved is final. Refused leaves the payment paid, and the merchant is told the refund failed. No answer is asked about later, the same way as a silent charge, and never sent twice. Beyond the cap, or after the window, the merchant cannot give money back alone. The bank does it instead. That is a refund by application. The merchant writes to the bank. One bank admin asks for the refund, and a second admin approves it. I think this is where a payments product shows its quality, not in how it takes money, but in how carefully it gives money back. Back to the wilted bouquet. In the merchant portal, Malika opens the paid payment. A box says refundable until, with the date, and how many days are left. She presses give back. A window asks: give the money back? It explains that the whole amount goes back to the buyer's card, reversed today, or refunded later. It asks for a reason, which both Malika and the bank will see. She confirms. The status turns to giving the money back. Moments later it says reversed, because it is still the same day. Under calls to the card scheme, a reversal line appears below the debit. On the buyer's page, it says money returned. Now Malika opens a hotel's large order, above the cap. There is no give back button. A grey note says above the refund limit, and that larger refunds are made by the bank on a written application. She opens an old payment from the spring. The note says the refund window has closed, and gives the date it could be refunded until. A teammate who only views sees no refund box at all. So Malika writes to the bank. In the admin portal, Dilnoza opens that old payment and chooses refund by application. She enters the application number and a note saying why. She presses send for approval, and the screen says it was sent to another admin. Only when a second admin approves does the money go back. So the claim stops double refunds. The logbook means every refund can be explained. The cap and the window protect Malika from large or late mistakes. And two admins protect the bank from one person moving money alone.
