Months after the bazaar, a letter comes to the bank. It is from Aziz. He writes that his phone was gone from Friday to Sunday. He asks if anyone used his account while the phone was gone. How can the bank answer him, and prove that the answer is true? Lesson nineteen showed the block by one operator, and the unblock by two. Lesson twenty showed the messages that the platform sent to Aziz on that weekend. Those messages are gone from his screen, but each step also left a record. This lesson shows that record, the audit trail, and how long the platform keeps each kind of data. Every step in the platform writes one audit event. An event says what happened, in which app, and at what time, to the millisecond. It says who acted: the customer, a staff member, or the platform itself. It names the device, the network address, the result and the reason. Registration, consent, the MyID check, the phone check, logins, blocks, operator actions and messages: each step is an event. But an event never holds a secret. It has no code, no password, no document image, no address and no full personal number. The personal number shows only its first two and its last two digits. So an event can travel to other teams without becoming a leak. Like the messages of lesson twenty, each event goes into the outbox of its module, in the same step as the change. Kafka carries the events to two readers. The first reader is the security monitoring of the bank, and it gets each event within seconds. If the monitoring is down, the events wait, and they arrive later in the correct order. The second reader is the audit module inside the platform, and it writes the audit trail. The audit trail only grows. No screen, no operator and no part of the application can change or delete an event. The database itself refuses a change or a delete in that table. But a person with full access to the database can switch that refusal off. So the trail also protects itself with a chain. The audit module gives each event the next number in the line. Then it seals the event with an HMAC, a fingerprint made with a secret key. The seal covers the number, the seal of the event before it, and the event itself. The key is the audit key, and it lives in Vault. Only the audit module can use it. This is an HMAC chain: each seal holds the seal before it. Change one old event, and its seal no longer fits. Fix that seal, and the next seal no longer fits, and so on to the end. Without the audit key, nobody can make new seals that fit. Every day, a check walks the chain from the start to the end. At the first break, it sends an alert to the platform team of the bank. Back to the letter. The security team of the bank finds the events of Aziz in its monitoring, from that Friday to that Sunday. Friday: an operator blocks the customer, with the reason phone lost. Every access grant of Aziz ends, and the message account blocked goes out. Over the weekend, the app on the lost phone asks for access, and the platform refuses it each time. No login with his number succeeds, and no money moves. Sunday: the first operator asks for an unblock, and a second operator approves it. The message account unblocked goes out, and Aziz opens the mobile bank again. The daily check found no break in those days, so nobody changed these events after that weekend. The bank writes back: between the block and the unblock, nobody used your account. Records like these do not stay forever. This is retention: each kind of data has its own keeping time, and the bank sets the long ones. A one-time code stays until it is used or expires, and then it goes. A registration that never reached level one goes when its time runs out, but its events stay. The customer record of Aziz and his verified identity stay while he is a customer. After a closure, they stay for the identity keeping time, and then they go. Audit events stay for the time that the regulation of the bank asks for, and then they go, one whole day at a time. Policy and text versions stay for the life of the installation. A retention job in the workers deletes what is due, from the database and, within the backup rotation, from the backups too. Nothing goes early, and when personal data goes, its events stay with masked references. Here is a question for you. A person with database access deletes one event at night, so how does the bank see it? The next daily check finds that the following seal no longer fits, and the platform team gets an alert. Each step writes one event, with no secret inside. The monitoring of the bank gets every event within seconds. The audit trail only grows, and its HMAC chain shows each change and each removal. Retention deletes personal data on time, and the events stay. All of this runs in the bank, on the machines of the bank.
