Six months after the stolen phone, a letter comes to the bank. Aziz writes that on that Friday night, before the block, someone approved a login on a laptop, and it was not him. He wants to know what happened. The bank wants to know too, and it also wants to show that its answer is the truth, not a story written afterwards. How does a platform keep a record that even its own administrators cannot rewrite? In lesson thirty-five, every message the platform sent left through the outbox, with its template version in an audit event. This lesson follows those audit events: what goes into one, why nobody can change it, who reads the stream, and how long each record stays. Start with one event. Each one has the event type, the app, the time to the millisecond, and the actor: a customer reference, a staff identity, or the system. It has the device, the network address, and the registration, session or case it belongs to. It has the result and a reason key, the MyID references when there are any, and, for a read of identity data, the reader and the purpose. The list of event types is long on purpose: registration, proofing, linking, devices, logins, administration, notifications, apps, sessions and resets. Now, what an event never has. No document images, no full document numbers, no full personal identification number, no addresses. No codes, no passwords, no result codes, no reusable identifiers, no access grants. The identifiers are the platform's own references, and the personal identification number shows only its first two and last two digits. So the trail can travel without becoming a leak. Second: nobody can change it. The platform only adds events. No operation of the system, and no administrator action, can change or delete an event before its retention ends. The database role of the application simply has no right to update or delete that table. But a person with direct access to the database could still try, so the trail protects itself with a chain. Each event gets an HMAC: a keyed fingerprint of its sequence number, the fingerprint of the event before it, and the event itself. The key lives in Vault, as the audit key of the installation. Change one old event, and its fingerprint no longer matches, and neither does any fingerprint after it. Every day a verifier walks the chain and raises an alert at the first break. I think this is what makes a trail worth keeping: we do not ask the bank to trust us, we let it check us. Third: who reads it. Each event goes to the security monitoring of the bank as a continuous feed, through a Kafka topic, within seconds. If the feed is down, the platform keeps the events and sends them later, in the correct order. A second stream goes to the bank's antifraud system: risk events, such as a login, a block or a password change, with references and no personal data. And Compliance can export events by period, by customer and by event type, in a format that a machine can read. Back to Aziz's letter. Compliance exports his events for that Friday. The trail shows a login on the marketplace web, a correct password, and an approval request to his trusted phone. It shows that the approval came from that phone, twenty minutes after the theft and half an hour before the block. It shows the block, and later the revoke of that same phone. So the approval came from the stolen phone, and the chain verifies, so nobody wrote these lines after the letter arrived. Fourth: how long each record lives. Every kind of record has its own clock, and the bank configures the long ones. A one-time code lives until it is used or expires, and then it is deleted. A registration that never reached level one is deleted after the registration-expiry period, but its audit events stay. An earlier version of a verified identity stays for the identity retention, counted from the day it was replaced. The customer and the current identity stay for the life of the customer, then for the identity retention after closure. Audit events stay for the audit retention that the bank's regulation asks for, and then they leave in groups of full days. Policy and text versions stay for the life of the installation. Deletion removes a record from the active store and, within the backup rotation, from every backup. Nothing is deleted early, and each month a deletion report shows that the rule held. To recap: one event for each step, with no secret inside it. An HMAC chain that the bank can check, a feed to its monitoring and its antifraud system, and a clock for every record. But someone had to export that Friday, and someone else pressed block. Who are these people, what may each of them do, and who checks them? Next: the administrator panel.
