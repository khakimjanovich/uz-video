Wednesday morning. The money from the card network lands, and the bank is short. Not by much, but nobody can say which payment caused it. On Tuesday night, one of Malika's payments timed out on our side, but the processing centre booked it. Another we marked paid, but the centre reversed it overnight. Both look fine in our own records. In lesson one, ecom checked each payment against the processing centre's own record, and in lesson thirty-four, it counted its own side of each day. This lesson puts the two sides next to each other, line by line. Every day, for each card network, the bank compares. This is reconciliation: proving that two sets of books agree, line by line. Our side is every money movement ecom recorded: each charge, each reversal, each refund. Their side is the processing centre's own report for that day. ecom fetches that report through the card zone, and it arrives with masked card numbers only. ecom does the matching; the card zone only hands over the report. Lines are paired by kind, by payment, and by the network's reference number. Each pair must then agree on four things: whether money moved, the amount, the terminal, and the day. The day's totals are checked too. Any difference is a break. A break is one line where the two books disagree, or a line that one side has and the other does not. A payment still unknown when the day closes is a break as well. Every break becomes exactly one exception: a case with an owner and a deadline. I think this is the part that matters most. A difference that nobody owns is a difference that nobody fixes. Then a person works it. If they find proof that the two sides really agree, they resolve it with that evidence. If the difference is real and cannot be recovered, they ask to write it off. A write-off means the bank accepts the difference and closes the case. Because a write-off is money the bank gives up, someone else must decide it. Every change to an exception is written down. Dilnoza opens Reconciliation in the bank's portal. She chooses a card network and Tuesday as the business day, and presses Reconcile. A run like this happens every night on its own; this button runs a day again. The run finishes. Totals shows Differ. The items list shows every line with its match state: matched, mismatched, or missing on one side. A mismatched line gives its reason: amount differs. Our amount and the network's amount sit side by side. Dilnoza opens Exceptions. Every unmatched item is here, with its owner and when it is due. One is marked Overdue. She presses Take, and the page says: you own this exception now. She adds a note about what she checked. The difference is real, so she writes the reason and chooses Ask to write off. The page says a second admin decides. When Dilnoza looks at the exception again, there is no approve button for her. Instead, a line says she asked to write this off, and another admin approves or rejects. A second admin logs in, opens the same exception, and reads the reason. They approve the write-off. The state becomes Written off. The audit trail at the bottom shows every step: who opened it, who took it, who asked, and who approved. Without reconciliation, two books can disagree for weeks, and the bank learns about it from a buyer's complaint. With it, Tuesday's difference is found on Wednesday, it has one owner and a deadline, and it cannot disappear because one person decided to ignore it. That protects the bank's money, and it protects Malika from a payment lost between two systems. Next: limits on what one website may take.
