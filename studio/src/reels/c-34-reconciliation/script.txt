How does the bank know its records match the card network's? In lesson one, ecom checked every payment against the processing centre's own record. In lesson thirty-four, ecom counted its own side of each day. This lesson puts the two sides next to each other, line by line. Picture two quiet mistakes. One payment timed out on our side, but the processing centre booked it. Another we marked paid, but the centre reversed it overnight. Both look fine in our own records. At settlement, the bank is short, and nobody knows which payment caused it. Think about checking your bank statement at home. You put it next to your own notes. You tick each line that matches. And you circle every line that does not. Every day, for each card network, the bank does the same. This is reconciliation: proving that two sets of books agree, line by line. Our side is every money movement ecom recorded: each charge, each reversal, each refund. Their side is the processing centre's own report for that day. ecom fetches that report through the card zone, and it arrives with masked card numbers only. ecom does the matching; the card zone only hands over the report. Lines are paired by kind, by payment, and by the network's reference number. Each pair must then agree on four things: whether money moved, the amount, the terminal, and the day. The day's totals are checked too. Any difference is a break. A break is one line where the two books disagree, or a line that one side has and the other does not. A payment still unknown when the day closes is a break as well. Every break becomes exactly one exception: a case with an owner and a deadline. Then a person works it. If they find proof that the two sides really agree, they resolve it with that evidence. If the difference is real and cannot be recovered, they ask to write it off. A write-off means the bank accepts the difference and closes the case. Because a write-off is money the bank gives up, someone else must decide it. Every change to an exception is written down. Let's watch the product. A bank admin opens Reconciliation in the bank's portal. They choose a card network and a business day, and press Reconcile. A run like this happens every night on its own; this button runs a day again. The run finishes. Totals shows Differ. The items list shows every line with its match state: matched, mismatched, or missing on one side. A mismatched line gives its reason: amount differs. Our amount and the network's amount sit side by side. The admin opens Exceptions. Every unmatched item is here, with its owner and when it is due. One is marked Overdue. The admin presses Take, and the page says: you own this exception now. They add a note about what they checked. The difference is real, so they write the reason and choose Ask to write off. The page says a second admin decides. When the same admin looks at the exception again, there is no approve button for them. Instead, a line says they asked to write this off, and another admin approves or rejects. A second admin logs in, opens the same exception, and reads the reason. They approve the write-off. The state becomes Written off. The audit trail at the bottom shows every step: who opened it, who took it, who asked, and who approved. What does this protect? Without reconciliation, two books can disagree for weeks, and the bank learns about it from a buyer's complaint. With it, every difference is found the next day. Every difference has one owner and a deadline. And no difference disappears because one person decided to ignore it. It protects the bank's money, and it protects the merchant from a payment lost between two systems. Back to the statement.
