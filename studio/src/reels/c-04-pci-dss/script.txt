Where may a card number live in your system? Picture a shop that keeps card numbers in its orders table, just in case it needs them for refunds. Then the numbers spread. A report reads them every morning. A backup copies them every night. And every developer's laptop can reach them. Now each of those systems holds card numbers, so each of them must be checked: its network, its logins, its logs and its changes. One weak laptop can leak every card the shop has ever seen. Those checks come from one rulebook. PCI DSS is the card industry's security rulebook, and it checks every system that touches a card number, or can reach one. So the way out is to have less to check. Think of a coat check. Coats go into one locked room, and everyone else gets a ticket. The ticket brings your coat back, but it is worth nothing to a thief. Card numbers work the same way: they go into one locked room, and every other system gets a ticket, the card token, never the number. In this system, the locked room is the card zone. It has its own database, its own keys and its own logins, and it shares none of them. It has only two doors: one for buyers' browsers, and one for partner systems like ecom. The card goes straight through the first door into the room, never through ecom. ecom only ever holds tickets, so a leak there leaks no card. ecom still gets a check, just a smaller one: it shows the card form, and it takes payments with tickets. Smaller, not none. In a bank, the card zone runs inside the bank's own secured network, with the bank's guards and firewalls around it. We deliver the locked room; the bank owns the building.
