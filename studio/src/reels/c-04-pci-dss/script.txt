Wednesday, Bekzod has an idea. Malika's shop could keep each buyer's card number in its orders table, just in case a refund ever needs it. In lesson three, the card number lived only in the card zone, and everything else got a mask or a token. This lesson shows why that rule exists, and how one locked room keeps its cost small. Bekzod's idea seems harmless. Then the numbers spread. A report reads the orders table every morning. A backup copies it every night. Bekzod's laptop holds a copy for testing. Now each of those places holds card numbers too, so each of them must be checked. One lost laptop can leak every card the shop has ever seen. Those checks come from one rulebook. PCI DSS is the card industry's security rulebook. It checks every system that stores, handles or sends a card number. It also checks every system connected to those, or able to change how safe they are. The check covers the system's network, its logins, its logs and its changes. So the report, the backup and Bekzod's laptop are all in the check now. The more places a card number can reach, the bigger the check, and the more ways to fail it. Most teams try to pass a big check. I think the better move is to shrink what gets checked. Put card numbers in one locked room. Give every other system a card token instead, never the number. In this system, that room is the card zone, and Timur runs it at the bank. It has its own database, its own keys and its own logins. It shares none of them with ecom. It has only two doors. One door is for buyers' browsers: the card form. The other door is for partner systems, like ecom. The database inside has no door at all. The room has no screen of its own, so follow one payment through it. A buyer opens the bank's payment page to pay for Malika's roses. The card form under "Card details" is the first door, opened inside the page. The buyer types the card, and it travels straight through that door into the room. ecom's servers are not on the way. Inside, the card is locked away, and a token comes back out. Then ecom knocks on the second door: it asks the room to charge the card, and it names the card only by its token. Timur looks at the running card zone. Two doors answer from outside. The database answers to nobody outside the room. And in the bank admin portal, Dilnoza sees only masked numbers. ecom holds tokens, never card numbers. Now the honest part. ecom still gets a check. It shows the card form on its page, and it uses tokens to take payments. So it is connected to the room: its check is smaller, but not none. And the room needs a building. The card zone runs inside the bank's own secured network. The bank's firewalls, guards and monitoring stand around it. The card zone fits in through its two narrow doors, and it shares no parts with the bank's other systems, so it breaks nothing around it. The product claims no certificate for itself. The bank's own assessor checks the room and the building together. So a leak from ecom leaks no card number at all. The buyer's card stays safe, ecom's check stays small, and the bank guards one small room instead of everything. Bekzod drops his idea. A refund needs the payment, not the card.
