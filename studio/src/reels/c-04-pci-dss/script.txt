Where may a card number live in your system, and what does that one choice decide? Take a shop whose checkout keeps card numbers in its orders table, for refunds. Reporting reads that table, a backup copies it every night, and every service and every developer's laptop can reach it. Ask where the card numbers are, and the honest answer is: everywhere the orders go. Every one of those systems now needs its network rules listed, its logins named, its logs kept for a year, and its changes controlled. A fee change waits for a card data review, and one weak laptop leaks every card. The standard itself gives the way out: keep card data in as few places as possible, and wall those places off. Then most of the system meets fewer requirements, and the part that meets all of them is small enough to get right. P C I D S S is the card schemes' security standard for anyone who stores, processes or transmits account data, and this course follows version four point zero point one. Its scope starts with the cardholder data environment, the C D E: every system that stores, processes or transmits that data. Any system with unrestricted connectivity to those systems belongs to the C D E too. Systems that could impact its security are in scope as well: authentication servers, key stores, logging, and name resolution. Without segmentation, that is the whole network. Segmentation isolates the C D E behind network security controls, N S Cs, such as firewalls that allow only the flows it needs. The standard does not require it, but it reduces scope, cost and risk. To be out of scope, a system must be isolated so well that, even if it is compromised, it cannot impact card data. In this system, the C D E is the cards unit: the card frame, the card vault, the BIN module, the adapters, and their infrastructure. This course calls it the card zone. The card zone shares nothing: it runs its own Postgres, Vault, Keycloak and observability, separate from ecom's. Anything it shared would be in scope too. Only two doors are open. Cards ingress is the door for payers' browsers, which load the card frame. Cards partner is the door for callers in other units, such as ecom. The card database is reachable from nowhere outside the unit, and lesson twelve covers both doors in detail. ecom never stores, processes or transmits a card number, so it is not in the C D E. It is still in scope, as a connected-to system and a security-impacting one: it calls the card zone, and its payment page embeds the card frame. The card zone shrinks ecom's scope without removing it, and lesson five draws that line. The standard groups twelve requirements under six goals. Every later lesson names the requirements it meets. Who meets them is split three ways. The product meets some in code: card numbers are sealed by Vault Transit before they reach Postgres. The bank meets others with its own zone, such as the physical security of the host and its media. Some are shared: Vault opens with three of five unseal shares, and the bank makes sure no one holds two. The bank's Qualified Security Assessor, a Q S A, judges all of it inside the bank's assessment, and from here on this course says the assessor. The product claims no certificate of its own, and lesson thirty-eight walks the full matrix. Now check the boundary on your own laptop. The implementation guide lists every flow the unit needs, and the unit uses nothing else, in or out. In: payers' browsers on the public port, callers' hosts on the door port, and the bank's jump host. Out: the card processing centres, the image registries, the mail relay, and the repository. Docker Compose publishes exactly those two doors, each on the address the environment names, and stops if the value is missing. The consoles, Keycloak, Mailpit and Grafana, listen on one two seven, zero, zero, one only. Postgres publishes no port at all: card data is read through the card vault only.
