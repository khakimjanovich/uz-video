Where may a card number live in your system? In lesson one, the card zone was the only place a card number ever lives. In lesson three, a card number was shown masked, kept as a token, and never copied around. This lesson explains why: the card industry's rulebook, and how one locked room keeps it small. Picture a shop that keeps card numbers in its orders table, just in case it needs them for refunds. It seems harmless. Then the numbers spread. A report reads the table every morning. A backup copies it every night. Every developer's laptop can reach it. Now each of those places holds card numbers too, so each of them must be checked. One weak laptop can leak every card the shop has ever seen. Those checks come from one rulebook. PCI DSS is the card industry's security rulebook. It checks every system that stores, handles or sends a card number. It also checks every system connected to those, or able to change how safe they are. The check covers the system's network, its logins, its logs and its changes. So in the shop's story, the report, the backup and every laptop are now in the check. The more places a card number can reach, the bigger the check, and the more ways to fail it. So the way out is to have less to check. Think of a coat check at a theatre. Coats go into one locked room. Everyone else gets a ticket. The ticket brings your coat back, but a thief who steals it gets no coat. Card numbers work the same way. They go into one locked room, and every other system gets a ticket: the card token, never the number. In this system, the locked room is the card zone. It has its own database, its own keys and its own logins. It shares none of them with ecom. It has only two doors. One door is for buyers' browsers: the card form. The other door is for partner systems, like ecom. The database inside has no door at all. Now watch the product. This room has no screen of its own, so watch how the system behaves. A buyer opens the bank's payment page. The card form under "Card details" is the first door, opened inside the page. The buyer types the card, and it travels straight through that door into the room. ecom's servers are not on the way. Inside the room, the card is locked away, and a token comes back out. Then ecom knocks on the second door: it asks the room to charge the card, and it names the card only by its token. Now look at the running card zone itself. Two doors answer from outside. The database answers to nobody outside the room. And in the bank admin portal, every payment shows only a masked number. ecom holds tickets, never coats. Now the honest part. ecom still gets a check. It shows the card form on its page, and it uses tickets to take payments. So it is connected to the room, and its check is smaller, but not none. And the room needs a building. In a bank, the card zone runs inside the bank's own secured network. The bank's firewalls, guards and monitoring stand around it. The card zone fits into that network through its two narrow doors, and it shares no parts with the bank's other systems, so it breaks nothing around it. The product claims no certificate for itself. The bank's own assessor checks the room and the building together. What does this protect? Without the room, every report, backup and laptop holds card numbers, and any one of them can leak them all. With the room, a leak from ecom leaks no card number at all. The buyer's card stays safe, ecom's check stays small, and the bank guards one small room instead of everything. Back to the coat check. Coats in one room, tickets everywhere else. We deliver the locked room; the bank owns the building. Next: how ecom takes a payment without ever seeing the card.
