Where may a card number live in your system? Picture a shop that keeps card numbers in its orders table, just in case it needs them for refunds. A card number is dangerous to keep, because anyone who has one can try to pay with it. Then the numbers spread. A report reads the orders table every morning. A backup copies it every night. And every developer's laptop can reach it. Now each of those systems holds card numbers, so each of them has to be checked: its network, its logins, its logs and its changes. Even a simple fee change now waits for a card data review. And it only takes one weak laptop to leak every card the shop has ever seen. Every new copy is one more place to protect, and one more place to forget. Those checks come from one rulebook. P C I D S S is the card industry's security rulebook, and it checks every system that touches a card number, or can reach one. So the way out is not to check everything, but to have less to check. Think of a coat check. Coats go into one locked room, and everyone else gets a ticket. With the ticket you can get your coat back, but the ticket itself is worth nothing to a thief. Only the attendant ever touches the coats. Card numbers work the same way: they go into one locked room, and every other system gets a ticket, the card token from lesson three, never the card number. Now only the room needs the full check. In this system, the locked room is the cards unit, and this course calls it the card zone. The card zone has its own database, its own keys, and its own logins. It shares none of them with ecom, because anything the room shared would need the full check too. The room has only two doors. One is for payers' browsers, which load the card form. The other is for partner systems, like ecom. When a payer types a card, it goes straight through the first door into the room, never through ecom. Nothing else gets in, and the database inside cannot be reached from outside at all. ecom only ever holds tickets. It asks the card zone to charge a card by its token, and the card number never leaves the room. ecom's database, reports, backups and laptops hold tickets too, so a leak there leaks no card. Even ecom's developers can follow a payment from end to end without ever seeing a card number. Here is the honest part. ecom still gets a check, just a smaller one. It shows the card form on its payment page, and it takes payments with tickets, so the rulebook still cares about it. Smaller, not none. Lesson five draws that line exactly: what ecom may hold, and what it may ask the card zone for. Now check the room on your own laptop. With both units running from lesson one, ask Docker Compose what the card zone publishes. The command lists each service with the ports it opens on your machine. Two doors are open, cards ingress and cards partner, and Postgres has no port on the outside at all. So card numbers live in one place, everything else holds tokens, and the check shrinks to fit. In a bank, the card zone runs inside the bank's own secured network, with the bank's guards, firewalls and checks around it. We deliver the locked room; the bank owns the building.
