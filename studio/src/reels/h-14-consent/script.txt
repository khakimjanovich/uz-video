It is Sunday evening, and Aziz wants a washing machine on credit. He opens the mobile bank, taps the credit offer, and the app stops him with a screen full of text. Before any camera opens, the screen tells him what the bank is about to read about him. Why does a screen come first, and not the face check? In lesson thirteen, Aziz's password became a digest that nobody can reverse, with a lock for wrong guesses. Everything so far proves his phone, not his person. This lesson adds the first step towards level two: the consent gate, the screen that stands before every call to MyID. Look at the screen with Aziz. It has four fixed parts. The first part is the data: his names, his date and place of birth, his personal identification number, gender, citizenship, nationality, document data, registered addresses and phone number. The second part is the provider: MyID. The third part is the purpose: to identify him as a customer of this bank. The fourth part says who can read the data later: the bank's channels and its operators. The bank writes the words of each part, in the language of the journey, but it cannot remove a part. And the screen shows only after Aziz's phone number is verified. Aziz reads the fourth line twice, and taps Refuse. What happens now? Nothing at MyID. The platform opens no session, counts no attempt, and keeps Aziz at level one. It writes one event: refused. I think this is the most honest choice in the whole proofing journey: saying no costs the customer nothing. Ten minutes later, Aziz changes his mind, and taps the credit offer again. The screen comes back. Nothing is pre-selected, and nothing is copied from his first answer. This time he taps Accept. Before any other step, the platform writes an acceptance record. It holds the identifiers and the versions of the consent text and the terms text, the language, the date and time, the device, and Aziz. Only then can the platform open a session at MyID. And one acceptance permits one session, no more. The record points to the session it allowed, so nobody can use a yes two times. The gate is not a one-time screen at registration. It stands before every call to MyID: a step-up, a new device, a dormant account, and a refresh. Next year, when Aziz moves to a new phone, he sees the gate again. Now the versions. Each consent text and each terms text exists in the four languages of the installation. Each version has a publication date, the staff user who proposed it, and a different staff user who approved it. The product ships a default text, the bank's compliance team can change it, and the platform keeps every version. Here is the case that this protects. Aziz opens the screen and starts to read, slowly. Two minutes later, compliance publishes a new version, with a changed purpose. Three minutes after that, Aziz taps Accept on the old text. The platform refuses the answer and shows him the new text. Why so strict? Because the acceptance record must name exactly the version that Aziz saw. If the bank must ever show what he agreed to, the record and the text must match, word for word. On the map, the consent step lives in the customer gateway, and the acceptance records live in the record store, next to the customer. The mobile bank only shows the screen and sends the answer. To recap: four fixed parts, one answer before every call to MyID, a refusal that costs nothing, and an acceptance record that names the exact version. Aziz has said yes. Now the camera can open.
