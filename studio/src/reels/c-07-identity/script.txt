How does the card zone know a call really comes from ecom? Picture a request that simply says, in its header: I am ecom. Anyone can type that line. A name you write yourself is like a name badge made at home: it proves nothing. Now picture the staff entrance of an office building. You show an ID card, issued by the building's own pass office. Reception gives you a day pass, and the pass lists the floors you may visit. The lift goes only to those floors. The card zone works the same way for every call: two proofs, from two places, and a list of floors. The first proof is a certificate: a digital ID card, signed by the card zone's own pass office. When a caller connects, it shows its certificate. The card zone shows its own certificate back. So each side knows who is on the other end. Because both sides show one, this is called mutual TLS. If a caller has no certificate from the card zone's pass office, the connection is refused before any request is even read. The second proof is an access token. Here, an access token is a day pass. The caller asks for it from the card zone's own login server, using its own secret. The card zone trusts only that login server. A pass from another product's login server is worth nothing here. Inside the access token is who it belongs to, and its roles. A role is a floor you may visit: one kind of operation. One role lets a caller bind a card. Another lets it read a card. Another lets it charge a card. Every operation needs its own role, and anything not listed is refused. So even the real ecom can only do what its roles allow. No caller at all holds the role for the card zone's admin operations. Now watch the product. These checks have no screen, so watch how the system behaves. A caller with no certificate knocks on the partner door. The connection closes at once. No request is read, and nothing is answered. Next, a caller brings a valid certificate, but a pass from the wrong login server. The card zone answers: unauthorized. The pass did not come from its own login server, so it counts for nothing. Now the real ecom calls, with both proofs. It asks to charge a card by its token. Its pass carries the role to charge, so the call goes through. Then ecom asks for an admin operation, one that searches cards. Both proofs pass. But ecom's pass has no admin role, so the card zone answers: forbidden. Three refusals, for three different reasons. No certificate means no connection. A bad pass means unauthorized. A missing role means forbidden. Each answer tells the operator exactly which check stopped the call. The card zone keeps its own pass office and its own login server. Neither is shared with ecom, so nothing outside the room can hand out the room's passes. What does this protect? Steal only the certificate, and you have no pass. Steal only the pass, and you cannot even connect. Steal both from ecom, and you still reach only ecom's floors, never the admin floor. And a mistake inside ecom cannot give ecom more power, because only the card zone grants the roles. This protects every card in the room, and the bank's trust that only its chosen callers can touch them. Back to the staff entrance.
