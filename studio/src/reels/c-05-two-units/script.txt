Saturday morning, Malika sells flowers at a market stall. A buyer pays by card. The card reader on her table belongs to the bank, not to her. The buyer types a PIN into the bank's machine, and Malika only gets a receipt. In lesson four, card numbers went into one locked room, and ecom kept only card tokens. This lesson shows how the bank puts that same card reader on its web payment page, so ecom never sees the card. Most teams build the obvious design first. A form on ecom's page sends the card number to ecom's server, and ecom passes it on. That one hop puts every card number through ecom. Then ecom's whole system needs the full check from lesson four. So this system copies Malika's stall. Six steps make it work. First, ecom and the card zone never just talk freely. They agree on every question they may ask each other, and every answer, written down once. That agreement is a contract. ecom can only ask what the contract allows, and the card zone answers only in the shape the contract says. Second, when a buyer opens the payment page, ecom asks the card zone for a card entry session. A card entry session is a short lived place to type one card, for one payment. The card zone answers with an address. Third, the payment page shows that address inside a frame. The frame is a small window inside the page, and it belongs to the card zone, not to ecom. That frame is the bank's card reader, standing on ecom's counter. Fourth, the buyer types the card into the frame. The card goes straight from the buyer's browser to the card zone. ecom's servers are not on the way. Fifth, the card zone locks the number away and hands the page a receipt: the card token, the masked number, and the buyer's bank. Never the number itself. Sixth, ecom asks the card zone to charge the card by its token. Before it does, ecom checks that this token really came from this payment's own session. Sunday evening, a buyer orders roses on Malika's site and lands on the bank's payment page. At the top it says "Secure payment page of the bank". It shows the order and the amount to pay. Below sits a box called "Card details": the frame, the bank's reader. The buyer types the card number and the date. The box hands back one short line: the masked number and the buyer's bank. The buyer types the code sent to their phone and confirms. The page says "Taking the payment", then "Your bank is being asked". A few seconds later it says "Paid", with a reference number. Another buyer leaves the page open and goes to make tea. Or gets the card wrong too many times. The frame says the attempt ended, too many tries or the time ran out, and asks for the card again. The session was short lived on purpose. Each session binds one card only, and then it is used up. In the bank admin portal, Dilnoza sees the roses payment with a masked number. That is all ecom ever holds: the token, the mask, the buyer's bank and the card network. So ecom's servers, logs and backups never hold a card number. A leak there leaks no card. Malika never touches the card either. And the bank keeps its full check on one small room. But the token still matters. It can start a payment, so ecom guards it with care. That is why ecom keeps its smaller check. On Saturday, the reader sat on Malika's table. On the web, it sits inside ecom's page. Either way, the shop keeps receipts, never cards.
