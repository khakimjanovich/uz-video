A Thursday in March. A buyer reads their card statement, sees Malika's flower shop, and does not remember any roses. They call their own bank and say: I never bought this. Weeks later, the claim reaches our bank through the card network, with a deadline attached. If the bank misses that deadline, the money is taken back without a fight. If Malika's evidence lands in the inbox of someone on holiday, the same thing happens. I think most disputes are not lost on the facts. They are lost on the calendar. In lesson thirty-two, the merchant chose to give money back, but here the buyer's bank, from lesson two, asks for it back instead. This lesson handles that claim with evidence, a deadline, and two admins, as in lesson eighteen. A dispute is a buyer's challenge to a payment that was already paid. It reaches the bank in one of two ways. Either the bank's own customer complains directly, or the buyer's bank sends a claim through the card network. Bank staff register it on the paid payment, for its whole amount. The payment becomes disputed, and the merchant is told at once. From there, the dispute moves through fixed steps: registered, evidence requested, answer sent, and then a result. Next comes the evidence date. Staff ask the merchant for evidence by a deadline counted in banking days. A banking day is a working day of the bank: weekends are skipped, and so are the bank's own holidays. The date must also leave a margin before the card network's deadline, so the bank has time to send its answer. The merchant uploads its files while the deadline holds. An upload cannot be taken back. Then the result. If the bank wins, the payment is simply paid again. If it loses, one admin records the loss, and a second admin must approve it. Only then does the dispute become a chargeback: the money goes back to the buyer. A loss means money leaving, so it always takes two people. There is one shortcut. If the payment is fully refunded before the answer is sent, the refund settles the dispute. Every step is written down: who did it, when, and why. And when disputes come close to their deadlines, an alert goes out. Dilnoza, in the bank's operations team, opens Payments in the bank's portal and picks Malika's paid payment. She chooses Register complaint. The form asks for the source: a complaint, or a claim from the card network. A claim needs its claim reference. She adds what the payer says, the day it was received, and the day the answer is due. The payment now shows Disputed, and Malika is told. Under Disputes, the list puts the nearest deadline first. One row is marked Due soon; another is marked Overdue, in red. Dilnoza opens Malika's dispute and chooses Ask for evidence. She writes what the shop should send, and picks a date. A date too close to the network's deadline is refused. In Malika's portal, the request appears, and she uploads the order and the courier's delivery note. Back in the bank's portal, the files show up under the dispute. Dilnoza marks the answer sent. Weeks later, the buyer's bank decides against the shop, and she records the result: lost. The payment does not change yet. A red banner says: Lost, waiting for a second admin. It names who recorded it, and when. The server refuses an approval from Dilnoza herself. A second admin logs in, reads the note, and approves the loss. The payment is now charged back. Malika lost one bouquet. She did not lose it to silence: she heard at once, she had real days to answer, and no single person could hand her money to the buyer. Next: checking our books against the card network's.
