What happens when a buyer says: I never bought this? In lesson thirty-two, the merchant chose to give money back. Sometimes the buyer's bank, from lesson two, asks for it back instead. This lesson handles that claim with evidence, a deadline, and two admins, as in lesson eighteen. The buyer tells their own bank. Weeks later, the claim reaches our bank through the card network, with a deadline attached. If the bank misses that deadline, the money is taken back without a fight. If the merchant's evidence lands in the inbox of someone on holiday, the same thing happens. Think of a small-claims court. It keeps one file for each case. It sets a date for your evidence, and that date skips the court's holidays. Then a judge rules, and the ruling goes in the file. A dispute works the same way. A dispute is a buyer's challenge to a payment that was already paid. It reaches the bank in one of two ways. Either the bank's own customer complains directly, or the buyer's bank sends a claim through the card network. Bank staff register it on the paid payment, for its whole amount. The payment becomes disputed, and the merchant is told at once. From there, the dispute moves through fixed steps: registered, evidence requested, answer sent, and then a result. Next comes the evidence date. Staff ask the merchant for evidence by a deadline counted in banking days. A banking day is a working day of the bank: weekends are skipped, and so are the bank's own holidays. The date must also leave a margin before the card network's deadline, so the bank has time to send its answer. The merchant uploads its files while the deadline holds. An upload cannot be taken back. Then the result. If the bank wins, the payment is simply paid again. If it loses, one admin records the loss, and a second admin must approve it. Only then does the dispute become a chargeback: the money goes back to the buyer. A loss means money leaving, so it always takes two people. There is one shortcut. If the payment is fully refunded before the answer is sent, the refund settles the dispute. Every step is written down: who did it, when, and why. And when disputes come close to their deadlines, an alert goes out. Let's watch the product. A bank admin opens Payments in the bank's portal and picks a paid payment. They choose Register complaint. The form asks for the source: a complaint, or a claim from the card network. A claim needs its claim reference. The admin adds what the payer says, the day it was received, and the day the answer is due. The payment now shows Disputed, and the merchant is told. Under Disputes, the list puts the nearest deadline first. One row is marked Due soon; another is marked Overdue, in red. The admin opens a dispute and chooses Ask for evidence. They write what the merchant should send, and pick a date. A date too close to the network's deadline is refused. In the merchant's portal, the request appears, and the merchant uploads its files. Back in the bank's portal, the files show up under the dispute. The admin marks the answer sent, and later records the result: lost. The payment does not change yet. A red banner says: Lost, waiting for a second admin. It names who recorded it, and when. The server refuses an approval from the same admin who recorded the loss. A second admin logs in, reads the note, and approves the loss. The payment is now charged back. What does this protect? It protects the bank from losing by silence: every deadline is visible, counted in real working days, and alerted. It protects the merchant, who always hears about a dispute and always gets time to answer. And it protects the money: no single person can hand a payment back to the buyer. Back to the court. One file for each case, a date that skips the holidays, and two people before any loss. Next: checking our books against the card network's.
