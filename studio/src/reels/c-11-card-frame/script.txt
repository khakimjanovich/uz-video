Bekzod builds Malika's website, and one Wednesday he reads about web skimming and stops sleeping well. A thief adds one line of script to a payment page. It might arrive through a hacked analytics tool, or a changed file on a shared server. That one line copies every card number as the buyer types it, and sends it away. The page still works. The buyer still pays. Nobody notices for weeks. Bekzod writes to Timur at the bank: could this happen to Malika's buyers? Timur answers with an invitation: come and try. In lesson five, the card frame became the bank's card reader on the payment page. This lesson shows the seals on that reader. The frame is the card zone's own small page, on its own address. It sits inside the bank's payment page like a window. A real card reader on a shop counter has tamper seals. The frame has three. Seal one: only the page its session names may hold the frame. Each time a buyer pays, the card zone opens a session for one page. A look-alike site that tries to hold the frame is refused by the browser. Seal two: a Content Security Policy. That is a rule the frame gives the browser. It says: run nothing but my own two files, and talk only to my own address. Any other script is refused before it runs. Seal three: Subresource Integrity. The frame names each of its two files by a fingerprint. If even one character of a file changes, the fingerprint no longer matches, and the browser will not run it. When the buyer finishes, the frame hands back only two things: a token and the masked number. It hands them only to the one page its session names. The card number itself never leaves the frame. When the browser blocks something, it reports it to the card zone. So a tampering attempt leaves a trace. And the page around the frame is watched too, because a skimmer there could still trick the buyer. Then Timur and Bekzod sit down to break it. First, a normal payment. The frame appears inside the payment page, with two fields: card number and expiry date. As Bekzod types, the number falls into groups of four. The expiry date gets its slash by itself. He presses Send code, types the code from his phone, and presses Confirm. The frame says "Card accepted" and clears the number field. The page shows only the masked line. Now Bekzod opens the frame's address on its own, outside the payment page. It shows one sentence: this page opens only inside the payment page. No form appears. He puts the frame inside a look-alike site. The browser shows an empty, refused box instead. Then he plays the thief. Inside the frame, he tries to load a script from another address. The browser's console shows a red line: the script is refused under the frame's policy. Nothing loads. Timur turns his screen around. The refusal has already arrived as a report. It sits in the card zone's logs, with the rule that was broken and the page it came from. No card data is in it. I think this is the part of card security most people forget. A database can be locked and sealed, and a skimmer can still copy numbers at the keyboard. The one moment a card number exists in plain view is in the buyer's browser, as it is typed. The seals guard that moment. They protect the buyer from a stranger's script. They protect the bank from a stolen form. And they protect Malika, whose pages never touch the number. That night, Bekzod slept. Next: the two doors into the locked room.
