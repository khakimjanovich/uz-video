Monday, seven in the morning. Timur stands in the bank's server room, in front of a fresh server. Today he installs the card zone, and five colleagues stand behind him. Dilnoza from operations is one of them. In lesson six, every key and setting in the system came from Vault. In lesson nine, card numbers were sealed with keys that never leave Vault. This lesson is about who can open Vault itself. There is an easier way, and Timur knows it. Run an install script once, and keep the master password in a text file on his laptop. Months later, Timur leaves the bank. Then a disk fails. The database backup is there, but nobody can read it, because nobody kept the keys that sealed it. One person and one file: the keys end up either lost or leaked. Most teams guard their keys against strangers, and forget the person who installed them. I think that person is the bigger risk. So Vault opens a different way. At install, Vault's opening key is split into five key shares. A key share is one piece of that key, useless on its own. Any three shares together open Vault. No one alone can, and no two can either. Each share goes to one person: a keyholder. A keyholder keeps their share in their own password manager, and nowhere else. The bank chooses the keyholders, and makes sure no one holds two shares. Nothing of this ceremony is written down anywhere: not the shares, not on the server, not in a log. Vault also locks itself. Every time it starts, it starts sealed: it holds everything, but can read nothing. To unseal it, three keyholders come, and each types their own share. Until then, nothing that needs Vault can run. Setting things up needs one more key: a master key for the session. It is made from three keyholders' shares, used for that one session, and always revoked at the end. So no master key sits around, waiting to be stolen. And backups. Card numbers in the database are sealed by Vault's keys. A database backup without that Vault is unreadable. So the database and Vault are always backed up together, and restored together. Back in the server room, Timur starts the card zone's installer. It checks the machine first, then reaches the keys. It says Vault is new, and that each of the five keyholders copies their own share straight into their own password manager. Then it waits: press Enter when the keyholders are here. Timur presses Enter. From here, the camera stays on the people, never on the screen. Each keyholder saves their own share. The installer waits again, until every share is saved. Then it clears the screen. Three keyholders step up, one at a time, and type their shares unseen: first to unseal Vault, then to make the session's master key. The installer sets everything up and starts the services. At the end, it revokes the master key. Then Timur does what every bank should do on day one. He restarts Vault, just as any reboot would. He runs the installer's check. It stops at once: Vault is sealed, and nothing that needs it can run. Three keyholders are called. Dilnoza types her share first, unseen. Two more follow. On the third share, Vault opens, and the check passes. Nine o'clock. No one in that room can open the keys alone, and no one can hold them hostage. If any one of them leaves, loses a laptop, or is tempted, the bank loses nothing. A stolen server or a stolen backup holds nothing readable. And as long as the database and Vault are backed up together, a restore works. Next: one install, everywhere.
