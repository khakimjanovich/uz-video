How do you make sure no single person can open the bank's secrets? In lesson six, every key and setting came from Vault. In lesson nine, card numbers were sealed with keys that never leave Vault. This lesson shows how Vault itself is opened, so no single person holds the bank's secrets. Picture a payments product installed by a script someone ran once. The master password sits in a text file on that person's laptop. Months later, that person leaves the bank. Then a disk fails. The database backup is there, but it cannot be read, because nobody kept the keys that sealed it. One person and one file: the keys end up either lost or leaked. Now think of a treasure map. Tear it into five pieces, and give the pieces to five different people. Any three of them can put the map back together. No one alone can, and no two can either. Vault, which holds every key and every setting in the system, opens the same way. At install, Vault's opening key is split into five key shares. A key share is one piece of that key, useless on its own. Any three shares together open Vault. Each share goes to one person: a keyholder. A keyholder keeps their share in their own password manager, and nowhere else. The bank chooses the keyholders, and makes sure no one holds two shares. Nothing of this ceremony is written down anywhere: not the shares, not on the server, not in a log. Vault also locks itself. Every time it starts, it starts sealed: it holds everything, but can read nothing. To unseal it, three keyholders come, and each types their own share. Until then, nothing that needs Vault can run. Setting things up needs one more key: a master key for the session. It is made from three keyholders' shares, used for that one session, and always revoked at the end. So no master key sits around, waiting to be stolen. Last, backups. Card numbers in the database are sealed by Vault's keys. A database backup without that Vault is unreadable. So the database and Vault are always backed up together, and restored together. Let's watch the product. An operator runs the card zone's installer on a fresh server. It checks the machine first, then reaches the keys. It says Vault is new, and that each of the five keyholders copies their own share straight into their own password manager. Then it waits: press Enter when the keyholders are here. Five people gather. From here, the camera stays on the people, never on the screen. Each person saves their own share. The installer waits again, until every share is saved. Then it clears the screen. Three keyholders step up, one at a time, and type their shares unseen: first to unseal Vault, then to make the session's master key. The installer sets everything up and starts the services. At the end, it revokes the master key. Now the operator restarts Vault, just as any reboot would. They run the installer's check. It stops at once: Vault is sealed, and nothing that needs it can run. Three keyholders are called. One by one, each types their own share, unseen. On the third share, Vault opens, and the check passes. What does this protect? It protects the bank from one person who leaves, loses a laptop, or is tempted. No one alone can open the keys, and no one alone can hold them hostage. It protects the card numbers too, because a stolen server or a stolen backup holds nothing readable. And it protects the bank's recovery: as long as the database and Vault are backed up together, a restore works. Back to the treasure map. Five pieces, five people, and three of them, every time the keys are opened. Next: one install, everywhere.
