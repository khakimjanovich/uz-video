It is Sunday evening, and Aziz sits at his laptop. He opens the marketplace web profile to see where his new kettle is. He types his phone number, then his password, and the password is right. But the page does not open his order. It asks him to approve the login in the mobile bank. The password is right, so why is it not enough? In lesson two, Aziz became one person, with one phone number, one password, and one session on each device. This lesson gives the three words that identity work uses for this moment, and it explains what a level really is. Each word answers a different question. The first word is authentication. Authentication asks: is this the owner of the account? Aziz answers with something he knows, the password, and something he has, his phone. On his phone, the mobile bank shows the request, with Approve and This was not me. In other cases, the phone gets a one-time code instead. Notice what authentication does not say. It does not say that this is Aziz from the state records. It says only that this is the person who registered the account. A stranger can register with their own phone and their own password, and pass authentication perfectly every day. The second word is identity proofing. Identity proofing asks: is this the real person in the state records? heeyID does it in one way only: MyID, in the master app. MyID compares a live face and a document with the state records. Aziz did it one time, in the mobile bank, last week. Proofing is slow and rare. Authentication is fast, and it happens at every login. I think the most common mistake in identity work is to treat a strong login as proof of a person. It is not. A password and a code prove that you hold the account, not who you are. The third word is authorisation. Authorisation asks: what may this session do, now? The marketplace must see level two before it shows personal data, like orders, addresses or cards. That is the rule that stopped the page. So what is a level? heeyID has three. Level zero: nothing is verified. Level one: a one-time code verified the phone number. Level two: MyID proved the person against the state records, and the phone number is confirmed. Only the check for a level gives that level. No operator and no setting can put a person at level two without MyID. Now the part that surprises most developers. A person has a level, and a session has a level too. Aziz, the person, is at level two. But a session gets level two only on a trusted device of a person at level two. A trusted device is a phone where the person passed MyID. His phone is one. A browser is never one. So the laptop session can reach level two in one way only: Aziz approves it in the master app, on his trusted phone. Back to Sunday evening. The request on his phone shows the browser, the network address and the time. Aziz taps Approve. The laptop session gets level two, and the order page opens. Now take a different evening. Aziz has no session in the mobile bank, so nobody can approve. After the password, the bank's SMS service sends him a code, and he types it. He is in: authentication passed. But the laptop session is at level one, and the order stays closed. The marketplace does not refuse him. It asks for a step-up: it names the level it needs, and the check that gives it. Same person, same password, two different answers. The difference is the session, not the person. Two more names, and you can read any identity document. OAuth 2.1 is the open standard for authorisation: how an app gets a token, and what the token lets it do. OpenID Connect sits on top of it, and adds authentication: who logged in, and how. heeyID has no token format of its own. Keycloak issues standard tokens, and each token carries the level of its session. So the marketplace reads the level from the token, and decides. To recap: authentication asks if this is the owner. Identity proofing asks if this is the real person. Authorisation asks what the session may do. A person has a level, and each session has its own. So why did the right password not open the order? Because a password answers only the first question. These checks touch passports and phone numbers. So where does heeyID run, and whom may it call? Next: the perimeter.
