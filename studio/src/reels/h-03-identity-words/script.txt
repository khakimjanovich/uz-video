On a Saturday, Aziz logs in to the mobile bank with his password and a code. The password is right, and the code is right. The mobile bank opens for him. He wants to send money to his mother. But the money does not move. His password and his code are both right, so why are they not enough for money? Lesson one showed this Saturday as one step of his journey, and it drew the map of the platform. This lesson gives the three words behind that step, and it explains what a level is. Each word checks a different thing. The first word is authentication. Authentication checks that this is the owner of the account. Aziz shows something he knows, his password, and something he has, his phone, which gets the code. Notice what authentication does not say. It does not say that this is Aziz from the state records. It says only that this is the person who registered the account. A stranger can register with his own phone and his own password, and he passes authentication every day. The second word is identity proofing. Identity proofing checks that this is a real person in the state records. heeyID does it in one way only: MyID, in the mobile bank. MyID compares a live face and a passport with the state records. Proofing is slow, and Aziz does it one time. Authentication is fast, and it happens at every login. The most common mistake in identity work is to treat a strong login as proof of a person. A password and a code prove that you hold the account, not who you are. The third word is authorisation. Authorisation decides what this session may do, now. The rule is simple: money data and the verified identity need level two. Cards, accounts and transfers are money data. The app's own data needs only level one. That rule stopped the transfer. heeyID has three levels. At level zero, nothing is verified. At level one, a code verified the phone number. At level two, MyID proved the person, and the phone number is confirmed as his. Only the check for a level gives that level. No operator and no setting puts a person at level two without MyID. A person has a level, and each session has a level too. A session in the mobile bank gets level two only on the trusted phone. The trusted phone is the one phone where Aziz passed MyID, and where TAF confirmed his SIM card. Only a session on that phone moves money. On that Saturday, Aziz is still at level one, and his phone is not trusted. The app does not refuse him. It names the level that it needs, and the check that gives that level. First his consent, then MyID, then the check of his phone number. Aziz passes all three, and he is at level two. His phone becomes the trusted phone, and the transfer to his mother goes. Same person, same password: the level and the phone make the difference. Keycloak issues the tokens for the app, and each token holds the level of its session. heeyID has no token format of its own. The tokens follow two open standards, and their names are in every identity document. OAuth 2.1 says how an app gets a token, and what the token lets it do. OpenID Connect sits on top of it, and adds who logged in, and how. The mobile bank reads the level of the session, and decides. So why did the right password and the right code not move his money? Because they pass only authentication, and money needs level two on the trusted phone. Authentication checks the owner of the account. Identity proofing checks the real person, one time, with MyID. Authorisation decides what each session may do, from its level. Today, Aziz is at level two, and his phone is the money phone.
