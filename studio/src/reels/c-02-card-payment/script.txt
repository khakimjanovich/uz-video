When a payment page says paid, has any money moved? Picture a corner shop with a tab book. You take your bread, and the shopkeeper writes one line in the book. No cash changes hands yet. At the end of the week, you settle up for every line at once. A card payment works the same way. Two banks take part in every card payment. The shop's bank is the acquirer. It signs up shops, takes their payments and pays them out, and this course builds its system. The buyer's bank is the issuer. It gave the buyer the card, and it holds the buyer's money. Between them sits the card network, which carries messages from one bank to the other. When the buyer presses Pay, the acquirer sends a question through the card network to the issuer: may this card pay this amount? The issuer checks the card and the money on the account. Then it answers yes or no, in a few seconds. That yes is a promise, written down. It is a line in the tab book, not money. The money moves later. Many payments travel together, in one batch, from the issuer through the card network to the acquirer. That batch is called settlement. Then the acquirer keeps its fee and pays the shop the rest. So a payment has two moments: the yes, in seconds, and the money, later. Giving money back depends on which side of settlement you stand. Before settlement, no money has moved yet. So the line is simply crossed out, as if the payment never happened. That is a reversal. After settlement, the money has already moved. Crossing out is too late, so the money must be sent back as a new movement. That is a refund. Now watch the product. On the bank's payment page, the buyer types the card and presses Pay. The page says "Taking the payment", and below it, "Your bank is being asked". A few seconds later it says "Paid", with a reference number. That is the issuer's yes. Now open the merchant portal, where the shop's staff work. The Payments page lists every payment on the shop's websites. Open the paid one. It shows the amount, the bank's fee, and every call to the card network. It also shows "Refundable until" a date, and a "Give back" button. Press it, and a dialog explains the rule in one line: on the day of payment the money is reversed; later it is refunded. In the bank admin portal, on its own Payments page, a payment given back shows one of two different states: Reversed, or Refunded. They never share one word. And each morning, the merchant's Daily reports page adds up the day before: what was paid, the bank fee, what was refunded, and what the shop receives. What does this protect? Picture a system that treats the yes as money. It pays the shop at once, then the batch never brings that payment, and the bank has paid out money it never got. Now picture a system that mixes reversal and refund. It crosses out a line that was already settled, and the buyer's money never comes back. Or it sends a refund for a payment that was already crossed out, and the buyer is paid twice. Either way, someone loses money: the bank, the shop or the buyer. So two pairs always stay apart. Approved is not paid out. A reversal is not a refund. Back to the tab book. A line is written in seconds. The money moves at the end of the week. Before then, you cross a line out; after then, you hand the cash back. Next: what you may keep about a card.
