When a payment page says paid, has any money moved? Picture a corner shop with a tab book. You take your bread, and the shopkeeper writes a line in the book. The money changes hands at the end of the week. A card payment works the same way. Two banks take part. The shop's bank is the acquirer, and this course builds its system. The buyer's bank is the issuer: it gave the buyer the card. When the buyer pays, the acquirer asks the issuer, through the card network, whether the payment is OK. The issuer checks the card and the money on the account, and says yes or no, in seconds. That yes is a line in the tab book, not money. The money moves later, in one batch for many payments, and that batch is called settlement. The acquirer keeps its fee and pays the shop the rest. Giving money back depends on timing. Before settlement, the line is simply crossed out, as if it never happened: a reversal. After settlement, the money has moved, so it has to be sent back: a refund. A system that mixes the two loses money, or pays twice. So two pairs stay apart: approved is not paid out, and a reversal is not a refund. Next: what you may keep about a card.
