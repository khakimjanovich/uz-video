Friday, nine in the evening. Malika runs a small flower shop online, and tonight it sells forty bouquets. Her phone buzzes forty times: paid, paid, paid. Saturday morning, she opens her bank account. Nothing is there. Nothing went wrong. Her phone told the truth, just not the truth she thought. In lesson one, a payment crossed the whole system and stopped at one word from the buyer's bank: yes. This lesson is about what that yes really is. Every card payment has two banks. Malika's bank is the acquirer: it signed her shop up, and it will pay her. The buyer's bank is the issuer: it gave the buyer the card, and it holds the buyer's money. Between them runs the card network, carrying one question: may this card pay this amount? The issuer checks the card and the balance, and answers in about two seconds. That yes is a promise. The issuer sets the money aside on the buyer's account. But nothing has moved. The money moves later, and in bulk. Overnight, the issuer sends the whole day's payments through the card network in one batch. That is settlement. Then the acquirer keeps its fee and pays Malika the rest. Her forty bouquets arrive as one transfer, on Monday. I think this is the most important idea in the whole course, and most teams building payments learn it the hard way: approved is not paid out. A system that pays the shop the moment it hears yes will one day pay for a payment that never settles. And the bank covers the gap. Sunday, a buyer writes to Malika: cancel my order. How the money goes back depends on one thing: has it settled yet? If the payment was made today, nothing has moved. The bank simply cancels the promise, as if the payment never happened. That is a reversal. Friday's payment has already settled. The money is in the batch, so it must travel back as a new movement. That is a refund. Mix them up and someone pays. Reverse a payment that already settled, and the buyer never sees their money again. Refund one that was already reversed, and the buyer is paid twice. That is why this product never uses one word for both. Here is how it looks. On the bank's payment page, the buyer confirms with a code from their phone. The page says "Your bank is being asked", then "Paid", with a reference number. That is the issuer's yes, nothing more. In the merchant portal, Malika opens that payment. She sees the amount, the bank's fee, every call to the card network, and "Refundable until" a date. She presses Give back, and the dialog tells her the rule in one line: today it is reversed, later it is refunded. The bank's staff see the same payment as Reversed or Refunded, never just "returned". Monday morning, Malika opens Daily reports. Friday is there: forty payments, the fee, and the payout that finally lands in her account. So when her phone says paid, it means: the money is promised. When the report says payout, it means: the money is yours. Next: what you may keep about a card, and what you never may.
