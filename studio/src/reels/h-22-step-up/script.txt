On Sunday evening, Aziz opens the marketplace app and taps My orders. He wants to see when the new kettle arrives. But Aziz is still at level one, and his orders are personal data. Should the marketplace simply say no? In lesson twenty-one, the token carried the session level, one or two. This lesson shows what an app does when that level is too low: the required level of each app, the step-up, and the trip to the master app. Start with the apps themselves. Each app of the bank is written in the installation with a name, a kind and a required level. The mobile bank is one app. The marketplace app is another, and the marketplace web profile is a third. The credit service is one more. The bank sets the required level of each app. In fact, the installation will not start until at least one app has a required level. An app with required level one is usable right after registration. Aziz registered, set his password, and could browse the marketplace at once. Now the rule that changes everything for an app team. Each app must ask for level two before it shows personal data: orders, addresses or cards. The app does not guess the level. It reads it from the identity summary, or straight from the token. So the marketplace sees level one, and My orders needs level two. Here is the part I think most teams get wrong. The easy answer is to refuse, with an error and a sad face. heeyID never refuses a customer here. Instead, the marketplace calls the step-up. The answer has three parts. The current level: one. The required level: two. And the checks that give level two: consent, a full proofing with MyID, and a phone check. So the answer is not no. It is the way up. But the marketplace is a side app. It has no MyID inside, and it never checks a face. So the step-up answer also says: continue in the mobile bank. If a side app tries to open a proofing anyway, the platform refuses that request. MyID runs only in the master app, because only the master app moves money and carries the strongest protection. The marketplace shows Aziz one button: continue in the mobile bank. He taps it, and the mobile bank opens. Now the checks run, one by one, and you have seen each of them already. First, the consent screen, from lesson fourteen. Aziz reads it and accepts. Then MyID, from lesson fifteen: his face and his passport. Then he confirms the data that MyID returned. Then the phone check, from lesson eighteen: his number against the numbers that MyID knows. Everything matches, and his status becomes Confirmed. Three things change at once. Aziz is now at level two. His phone becomes Trusted. And the gateway issues a new mobile bank token with level two. Notice what did not happen. No app gave Aziz level two. No approval gave it, and no operator gave it. Only the MyID check gives level two. That is a hard rule of the platform. The level belongs to the person, so it is the same in every app. But a session has its own level, and that is a different thing. In the mobile bank, a session has level two only on a trusted device. If Aziz opens the mobile bank on a different phone, that session starts at level one. In a side app, a session gets level two only after Aziz approves it in the master app. Lesson twenty-six shows that approval. So back in the marketplace, the person is at level two, and the marketplace session reaches level two through that approval. I think this is a good design. The person proves who he is once, in one strong place. Every other app borrows that proof, and never copies it. To recap. Each app has a required level. When the session level is too low, the platform does not refuse. It answers with the level and the checks. And the checks run only in the master app. Tonight, Aziz logs out of the mobile bank on his trusted phone. Tomorrow morning, on the same phone, his session is gone. How does he get back in?
