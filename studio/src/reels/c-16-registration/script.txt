Before Malika sold a single bouquet online, she sat at her kitchen table one Sunday night and signed her flower shop up with the bank. Lesson fifteen finished the card zone, so from here the course builds the shop's side, starting with the portal that Malika and the bank's staff used in lesson one. This lesson is about her first five minutes in it. The page said "Create a merchant account", and under it, "We will email you a code to confirm the address." There were three boxes: company name, work email and password. She filled them in and pressed Create account. Her password went straight to the login server, the one place that checks passwords. It never touched ecom's own database. ecom saved her shop as pending, and nothing more. Then her phone buzzed. An email had arrived, with six digits in it. Why a code? Because anyone can type anyone's email into a sign-up form. A stranger could open a shop with Malika's address, and the bank would send contracts, reports and payout notices to an inbox whose owner never asked for any of it. The code asks one thing: is this inbox really yours? It is a one-time code: a short number that works once, for a short time. Only someone who can read that inbox can type it back. The store that keeps the codes never keeps the code itself, or the email. It keeps only fingerprints of them: enough to check a match, useless to anyone who steals them. In the portal, the page now said "Check your email." It asked for the code, and said it works for a few minutes. While the box was not full, the Verify button stayed grey. Under it, "Send a new code" counted down the seconds before it could be pressed again, so nobody can flood an inbox. Malika typed the six digits, and Verify lit up. One click. The page said "Email verified", told her to sign in, and said the bank will now check her documents. That right code is email verification: proof that the person controls the address. Behind that screen, Malika became her shop's first admin, both in ecom and on the login server. And the stranger? Their wrong guesses are counted. Too many, and the sign-up is locked until the window ends, even if a new code is asked for. That is a lockout. Every refusal says when to try again. One more case, and it is the one I care about most. If the store that keeps the codes is down, nothing is sent and nothing is accepted. Most teams get this wrong under pressure: sign-ups are stuck, and someone lets them through just this once. That is exactly how an unchecked shop gets in. So the system fails closed, never open. The code protects the real owner of the address. It protects the bank too, because every later message reaches the person who really asked. Malika never noticed any of it. She saw six digits, and a green tick. Next: how her shop earns the right to take money.
