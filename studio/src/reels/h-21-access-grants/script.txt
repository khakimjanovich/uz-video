It is Sunday, the day after the step-up, and Aziz opens the mobile bank at breakfast to check his balance. He opens it again at lunch, and once more at night. He never types his password. So what lets him in, again and again, without a login? Lesson sixteen made this phone his money phone, with an app PIN and a binding check at each request. Now look at what the phone carries between two logins. It carries an access grant. An access grant is a session: one app account, on one device, for one customer. Aziz got his first grant on registration day, the Sunday before, at level one. He gets a new grant after each login. Two parts of the platform make a grant, and they have very different jobs. The customer gateway decides. It checks the password, the code, the verdict of TAF and the device. Keycloak only issues. The app never calls Keycloak, since it talks only to the gateway. When the gateway is satisfied, it signs a short note for Keycloak. The note says three things: this device, this customer, this level. It is valid for a very short time, and only once. Keycloak reads the note and issues two tokens: an access token and a refresh token. Keycloak is a well-known engine for tokens, but it knows nothing about TAF or MyID. So all the rules of the bank stay in the gateway, and a new version of Keycloak never changes a decision. Now read the access token. First, it names its app two times: who the token is for, and which app asked for it. Here, both names say the mobile bank. The backend of the mobile bank accepts only tokens that were issued for the mobile bank. A token for any other app gets one answer: session of a different app, log in again. The app comes from the signed token, never from a header that anyone can write. Second, the token carries the level of the session: one or two. The gateway writes level two only for a customer at level two, on his trusted phone. On registration day, Aziz's token said level one. On Saturday he passed MyID on this phone, and the gateway issued a new token with level two at once. He did not wait for the old token to run out. The token also says whether this phone is the money phone. So the backend of the mobile bank reads the level and the money phone straight from the token, with no extra call. Back to breakfast. The access token lives only for a short, configured time, on purpose. When it runs out, the app asks Aziz for nothing. It sends the refresh token to the gateway, with its device identifier. This is a renewal. The gateway renews only for the same device and the same app. First, the renewal passes the binding check from lesson sixteen. Then the gateway looks at its list of ended grants, at each call. When both checks pass, Keycloak gives a fresh access token, and a fresh refresh token too. The old refresh token is now used up, and Keycloak sees it if it ever comes back. A renewal needs no password. The password opens a login, and a renewal only continues one. That is also why the password lock of lesson eleven ends no grant. There is one more limit: inactivity. When Aziz leaves the app alone for longer than the configured limit, the app asks for his app PIN. The grant does not end, and the PIN only unlocks the app on this phone. The full lifetime of a grant and the inactivity limit are policies, and they come from the installation configuration. Now the other side: how a grant ends. First, its lifetime runs out. Second, the bank blocks the customer, as lesson nineteen shows on the day Aziz loses his phone. The grant on the phone then ends at its very next request, not at its next renewal. Third, a binding mismatch: the gateway revokes the device, and its grant ends at that request. Fourth, a closure of the customer ends every grant he has. In each case, the app gets the same answer: this grant has ended. The app tries a renewal, and when the renewal fails too, Aziz must log in again. Now a check for you. At lunch, after three quiet hours, what does Aziz type: his password, his app PIN, or nothing? His app PIN, because the app was idle past the limit, while the grant goes on and its renewal needs no password. The gateway decides, and Keycloak issues. The token names its app and carries the level. A renewal keeps the session alive with no password. And an ended grant stops at the very next request. On Monday his nephew wants the phone, so Aziz logs out, and lesson eighteen shows how he logs in again on the same phone.
