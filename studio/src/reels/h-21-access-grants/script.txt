Aziz opens the mobile bank at breakfast to check his balance. He opens it again at lunch, and once more at night. He never types his password. So what lets him in, again and again, without a login? In lesson twenty, his phone got a record and a state, and the gateway checked the binding at each request. This lesson shows what the app holds when it calls: the access grant, its tokens, its level and its renewal. An access grant is a session. It belongs to one app account, on one device, for one customer. Aziz got his first grant on registration day, at level one. He gets a new one after each login. Two parts of the platform take part, and they have very different jobs. The customer gateway decides. It checks the password, the code, the verdict and the device binding. Keycloak only issues. When the gateway is satisfied, it signs a short note for Keycloak. The note says three things: this device, this customer, this session level. It is valid for a very short time, and only once. Keycloak reads the note and issues two tokens: an access token and a refresh token. I think this is the right split. Keycloak is a well-known engine for tokens, but it knows nothing about TAF, MyID or the bank's rules. All those rules stay in the gateway, so a new version of Keycloak never changes how the bank decides. Now look at what the access token says. First, it names its own app. A mobile bank token never opens the marketplace, as lesson five showed. Second, it carries the session level: one or two. The gateway writes level two only when the customer is at level two and the device is Trusted. On registration day, Aziz's token said level one. A week later he passed MyID on this phone, and the gateway issued a new token with level two at once. He did not wait for the old token to run out. So any app, and any backend of the bank, can read the level straight from the token. Back to breakfast. The access token lives only a short time, on purpose. When it runs out, the app does not ask Aziz for anything. It sends the refresh token to the gateway, with its device identifier. This is a renewal. The gateway renews only for the same device and the same app. Before anything else, the renewal passes the binding check from lesson twenty. Then the gateway looks at the revocation list. This list holds every grant that was ended, and the gateway reads it at each call. If both checks pass, Keycloak gives a fresh access token, and a fresh refresh token too. The old refresh token is now used up. If someone tries to use it a second time, Keycloak sees the reuse. A renewal needs no password. The password opens a login, and a renewal is not a login. It only continues one. There is one more limit. When Aziz leaves the app alone for longer than the inactivity limit, the app asks for his app PIN. The grant does not end. The PIN only unlocks the app on this phone again. The bank sets the full lifetime of a grant, and its inactivity limit, as policies. Now the other side: how a grant ends. Last year Aziz used the mobile bank on a tablet. This week he cannot find it. He calls the bank, and an operator revokes the tablet. The grant of the tablet ends at its next request, not at its next renewal. If the tablet calls, it is refused. If it tries to renew, the revocation list says no. A grant can end in a few more ways: a block of the customer, a binding mismatch, a replaced phone, a phone number that was removed, a password reset or change on a different device, or simply its lifetime. In each case, the app gets the same answer: this grant has ended. The app tries a renewal. When the renewal fails too, Aziz must log in again. To recap. The gateway decides, and Keycloak issues. The token names its app and its level. A renewal keeps the session alive with no password. And one revocation stops a device at its very next request. But look at the marketplace. Aziz's marketplace token says level one, and the page he wants shows his orders, which needs level two. Should the marketplace refuse him? What does an app do with a level that is too low?
