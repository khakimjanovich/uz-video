It is Tuesday, ten past one, and Aziz orders a kettle from the marketplace, on his work laptop. That morning he paid his phone bill in the mobile bank, and checked a parcel in the marketplace app on his phone. Three doors into one bank, on two devices. So how many identities does Aziz have? In lesson one, the map showed the apps, the gateway, Keycloak and the stores. This lesson looks inside one box on that map: the customer, and how one person stays one person in every app. Start with the model that most teams build first. Each app keeps its own customers, with their own passwords. The mobile bank has an Aziz. The marketplace has an Aziz. The credit service has an Aziz. Each one has a password, and each one has a level. Aziz passes MyID in the mobile bank, and that Aziz is at level two. But the marketplace Aziz is still at level one, so the marketplace asks for proof again. Now a bank operator sees a strange login in the marketplace. She wants to end his marketplace sessions, and only those. In this model she cannot: the sessions do not know their app, and the three Azizes do not know each other. I think this is where most identity bugs start: one person, three passwords to steal, and three levels that disagree. heeyID turns the picture around. At the centre is one person. The person record keeps the personal identification number, the level and the verified identity. Under it is one phone number. The phone number is the login for every app. Next to it is one password. The apps have no passwords of their own, so a password change applies in every app at once. The level belongs to the person, so it is the same in every app. Now the apps. Each app keeps one account of the person: an app account. The platform makes it the first time Aziz registers or logs in to that app. Aziz has three: the mobile bank, the marketplace and the credit service. Each app account has its sessions, one for each phone or browser. In the mobile bank, Aziz has one session, on his phone. In the marketplace, he has two: one on his phone, and one in the browser on his laptop. Each session records its app and its device. Its tokens are for its own app only, so a marketplace token never opens the mobile bank. Lesson five shows how Keycloak does this. Back to the operator. Now she has three choices. She can end one session: the laptop. She can end all the sessions of one app: the whole marketplace. Or she can end all the sessions of the person. Every app keeps working, and the level does not move. One more thing sits next to the phone number: a log of every check. A code from the bank's SMS service, in the marketplace, at nine fifteen. A TAF check, in the mobile bank, at nine twenty. A MyID check, in the mobile bank, last week. Each line has the provider, the app and the time. So the bank always knows how it knows that this number is Aziz's. But not every app may do every check. The mobile bank is the master app. It moves money, so only the master app runs MyID, TAF and the app self-protection. Every other app is a side app: the marketplace app, the marketplace web profile, and the ordering, delivery and service apps. A side app never checks a face. In a side app, Aziz types his phone number and his password. Then he approves the login in the master app, or he types an SMS code from the bank's SMS service. A customer can even use the side apps with no account in the master app at all. Now draw a frame around everything you see: one installation. One installation serves one bank, with one Keycloak realm, and each app is one client in it. A second bank gets its own installation, so no request can ever read across two banks. To recap: one person, one phone number, one password. One account in each app, and one session on each device. Only the master app proves who he is. So how many identities does Aziz have? One. But look at the laptop again. Aziz is at level two, yet his new laptop session starts at level one, until he approves it in the mobile bank. So a session has a level too. What does level really mean?
