Monday, ten fourteen in the morning. A buyer orders peonies from Malika's shop, types a card, and pays. It takes under a minute. In that minute, one card number passes seven layers of security, and the buyer notices none of them. Since lesson four, each lesson has added one layer around the card zone. This lesson follows that one card number through all of them, and shows what each one stops. A bank vault is the right picture. A gate on the street. Glass at the counter. A guard inside. A safe at the back. Inner doors that open only with the right pass. Cameras everywhere. And keys no single person holds. No layer is trusted to stop everything. Each one assumes the layer before it might fail. That idea is called defence in depth. Layer one is the gate: the payers' door. The buyer's browser reaches the card zone only through this door. It passes the card frame's few requests, and answers not found to everything else. It slows any one buyer who submits too fast. Layer two is the glass at the counter: the card form's seals. The frame runs only its own two files. A changed file does not run. A stranger's script is refused. Only the payment page its session names may hold it. This stops a skimmer copying the number as it is typed. Layer three is the guard: the fraud limits. Before any network is asked, the attempt is counted by the card's fingerprint, its first digits, the buyer's address and the shop. Past the bank's chosen limits, the try is refused. This stops a thief testing stolen cards. Layer four is the safe: sealed numbers. Vault seals the number, and only the sealed value is kept. The database refuses anything unsealed. The number is opened only to hand it to the card's network. This stops a stolen database or backup from leaking a single card. Layer five is the inner doors: two proofs on every call. When ecom asks to charge the card, it never sees the number. It holds only the token. Every call it makes carries two proofs: its certificate, and its token from the login server. Its role allows only the operations it needs. This stops a stranger, or a caller overstepping, from using the card. Layer six is the cameras: logs that never show the number. Every use of card data is written down. Every log line cuts a card number down to its first six and last four digits. Secrets in a log are starred out. Alerts carry no card data at all. This stops the record of what happened from becoming a leak. Layer seven is the keyholders. Vault itself starts locked. To open it, three of five keyholders each bring their own share of the key. This stops one insider from taking the keys. Now the buyer's minute, as they saw it. The payment page opens, and the padlock shows. The frame appears and groups the number in fours. The buyer presses Send code, and a code arrives on their own phone. They confirm, and the form says "Card accepted". The page shows only the masked line, the network and the bank. ecom asks for the charge by token. The page says "Paid". Seven layers worked, and the buyer saw two fields and a button. Later, Timur opens the card zone's logs for that payment. He sees only the masked number. He also sees who asked for what, and when. That night, Vault restarts after maintenance. It comes back locked. The card form refuses new cards, and an alert says a service the card zone depends on is unavailable. It stays that way until the keyholders meet and open it. Timur cannot open it alone, and that is exactly the point. I trust no single layer, not even my favourite ones. One day a script slips past review. A password leaks. A copy of the database escapes. When one layer fails, the next one still holds, for the cardholders, for shops like Malika's, and for the bank. Next: how a merchant signs up.
