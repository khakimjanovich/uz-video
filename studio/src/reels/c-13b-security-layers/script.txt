How deep does the security around one card number really go? Since lesson four, each lesson added one layer to the locked room: its doors, its frame, its proofs, its sealed numbers and its limits. This lesson follows one card number through every layer, and shows what each one stops. Think of a bank vault. There is a gate on the street. A guard inside the door. Inner doors that open only with the right pass. A safe at the back, with keys no single person holds. And cameras everywhere. No one layer is trusted to stop everything. Each layer assumes the one before it might fail. That idea is called defence in depth. The card zone is built the same way. Follow one card number through it, layer by layer. Layer one is the gate: the payers' door. The buyer's browser reaches the card zone only through this door. It passes the card frame's few requests and answers not found to everything else. It slows any one buyer who submits too fast. Layer two is the glass at the counter: the card form's seals. The frame runs only its own two files. A changed file does not run. A stranger's script is refused. Only the payment page its session names may hold it. This stops a skimmer copying the number as it is typed. Layer three is the guard: the fraud limits. Before any network is asked, the attempt is counted by the card's fingerprint, its first digits, the buyer's address and the shop. Past the bank's chosen limits, the try is refused. This stops a thief testing stolen cards. Layer four is the safe: sealed numbers. Vault seals the number, and only the sealed value is kept. The database refuses anything unsealed. The number is opened only to hand it to the card's network. This stops a stolen database or backup from leaking a single card. Layer five is the inner doors: two proofs on every call. When ecom asks to charge the card, it never sees the number. It holds only the token. Every call it makes carries two proofs: its certificate, and its token from the login server. Its role allows only the operations it needs. This stops a stranger, or a caller overstepping, from using the card. Layer six is the cameras: logs that never show the number. Every use of card data is written down. Every log line cuts a card number down to its first six and last four digits. Secrets in a log are starred out. Alerts carry no card data at all. This stops the record of what happened from becoming a leak. Layer seven is the keyholders. Vault itself starts locked. To open it, three of five keyholders each bring their own share of the key. No one person can open the safe alone. This stops one insider from taking the keys. Now watch the product, with one buyer. The buyer opens the payment page. The padlock shows. The frame appears and groups the number in fours. The buyer presses "Send code", and a code arrives on their own phone. They confirm, and the form says "Card accepted". The page shows only the masked line, the network and the bank. ecom asks for the charge by token. The page says "Paid". Seven layers worked, and the buyer saw two fields and a button. On the bank's side, the team opens the card zone's logs for that payment. They see the masked number, never the whole one. They see who asked for what, and when. Now Vault restarts. It comes back locked. The card form refuses new cards, and an alert says a service the card zone depends on is unavailable. It stays that way until the keyholders meet and open it. What does this protect? Any single layer can fail one day. A script slips past review. A password leaks. A copy of the database escapes. When one layer fails, the next one still holds. That protects cardholders, shops and the bank. Back to the vault. Gate, guard, inner doors, safe, keyholders and cameras. A thief must beat every one of them. Each one only has to hold.
