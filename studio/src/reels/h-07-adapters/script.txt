At ten past six on a Friday, Aziz stands in the mobile bank, ready for his MyID check. At the same minute, the MyID service starts to answer slowly, then not at all. Aziz looks at a spinner. What happens to him, and what happens to the platform? In lesson six, some configured values were timeouts: how long heeyID waits for MyID, TAF or the SMS service. This lesson shows the walls around every outside call: ports, adapters and breakers. Start in the middle. The domain of heeyID holds the rules: registration, proofing, linking, devices and login. The domain has no input and no output of its own. It never opens a connection, never reads a file, and never calls MyID. When it needs something from outside, it calls a port. A port is an interface in the words of the domain: open a proofing session, send a code, find a customer by personal identification number. An adapter is the code behind the port that talks to one real service. heeyID has eight outgoing ports: MyID, TAF, the SMS service, the push service, the customer lookup, the antifraud system, the staff identity and the security monitoring of the bank. Each port has two implementations: the real adapter, and a simulator. The simulator plays the service, with every answer that the contract allows: success, each failure reason, a timeout and a down mode. The acceptance tests run against the simulators, with no internet. A nightly run uses the MyID test environment. I think the simulator is the part that most teams skip, and then regret. Without it, you test only the good days. Now the walls. Each outgoing call has a timeout, and the timeout is a policy. Each adapter also has a breaker. After a configured number of failures in a row, the breaker opens: the dependency is down, and calls fail at once, with no waiting. A probe checks the service at a configured interval, and closes the breaker when the service answers again. While the breaker is open, an alert tells operations that a dependency is down. And now the most important rule: each dependency has one fixed behaviour when it is down. The adapter makes that behaviour impossible to bypass. A timeout always gives a named outcome, never an exception that each caller can read in its own way. Here are the fixed behaviours. MyID is down: the customer stays at the current level. The attempt does not count against the limits, and no proofing happens later in the background. TAF is down: the codes go through the SMS service of the bank, and the attempt is not scored. There is one exception: when the state services portal refuses this bank as a trusted bank, there is no fallback. The SMS service is down: no code can go, and the customer sees that the service is not available. Notifications wait in Kafka, and go again later. The push service is down: the approval request waits for the app to ask for it, and notifications go by SMS only. The customer lookup is down: the link waits as pending, a worker tries again during the configured period, and the step-up still completes. The antifraud system is down: its events wait in Kafka, and no journey waits for antifraud. Back to Aziz and his spinner. The MyID adapter waits the configured time, then gives a named outcome: the provider is not available. Aziz sees a plain message, and he stays at level one. His attempt does not count. The failures continue, so the breaker opens, and the next customers get the same answer at once, with no spinner. The panel shows the status of the provider. When MyID comes back, the probe closes the breaker, and Aziz tries again. Two more rules keep adapters safe. First, no adapter writes a request body or a response body to a log. It writes the reference, the outcome and the time that the call took. Second, hosts, ports and credentials come from the installation configuration and the key store, never from the code. The MyID adapter and the push adapter even run as their own services, so only they hold the MyID credential and the push credential. To recap: a domain with no input and no output. A port for each outside service, with a real adapter and a simulator. A timeout and a breaker on every call, and one fixed behaviour for each outage. So Aziz waits a few seconds, not forever. But each adapter holds a secret: the MyID credential, the TAF client secret, the push credential. And the platform keeps passports encrypted. Where do all those keys live? Next: keys.
