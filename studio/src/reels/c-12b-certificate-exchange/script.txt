The message reaches Timur on a Friday afternoon. An engineer on the ecom side has made a private key for ecom's calls into the card zone. Shall I just email it over, he asks? Timur's reply is one word: never. In lesson twelve, a caller passed the mail slot only with a certificate from the card zone. This lesson shows how a caller gets that certificate, while its secret key stays at home. Think about what that email would do. One product's secret would sit in another product's inbox. Whoever holds that key can pretend to be the caller. I think a private key that has travelled is a key you no longer own. The right way works like a passport application. You send a photo, the office stamps it, and it never needs your face. First, the caller makes its private key inside its own Vault. The key stays there, and never leaves. From that key, the caller makes an application form. The form carries the key's public half and the caller's name. That form is called a certificate signing request. It is the only thing the caller sends. On the other side sits the card zone's certificate authority: the one the card zone trusts to stamp certificates. It checks the form. Is it well made? Does it carry the right name? Is that name on the card zone's list of callers? If so, it signs it, for a limited time. The signed certificate is good only for calling in, never for serving. Then one folder goes back to the caller, with four things inside. The signed certificate. The authority's own seal, so the caller can recognise the real card zone. A secret for the login server, which travels by its own secure channel. And the addresses to call. The caller stores everything in its own Vault. Then it deletes the loose secret file. From then on, every call it makes carries two proofs: the certificate, and a token from the login server. And every certificate is watched, so a warning goes out well before it expires. There is no portal for this, so here is what the two operators did that Friday. On the ecom side, the installer made the key and wrote out one small form. The engineer carried that form, and only that form, to Timur. Timur signed it. Had the name not been a listed caller, the signing would have stopped with a clear refusal, and nothing would have been issued. The name was listed, so a folder appeared, named for ecom, with the four things inside. The engineer carried the folder back. The secret went separately, by a secure channel. On the ecom side, the installer read the folder. It wrote it all into ecom's Vault, and removed the secret file from disk. The first call went through the mail slot, and the card zone answered. Months later, Timur's team sees a warning that the certificate is close to its end date. They repeat the same steps, and the old certificate retires on time. Nobody is woken by a sudden outage. Each product keeps its own key, in its own Vault. A leak on one side never exposes the other. The card zone decides who may call, and for how long. The caller never touches the card zone's keys. And for the bank's audit, every certificate has one owner, one signer, and one end date. Next: how the card zone stops a thief testing stolen cards.
