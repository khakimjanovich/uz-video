How does ecom get a certificate from the card zone without handing over its secret key? Start with the wrong way. One team makes a private key for the caller, and emails it across. Now one product's secret sits in another product's inbox. Whoever holds that key can pretend to be the caller. So the key must never cross. Think of a passport office. To get a passport, you send an application with your photo. The office checks it and stamps it. It never needs your face, only the photo. A caller does the same. First, it makes its private key inside its own Vault. The key stays there, and never leaves. From that key, the caller makes an application form. The form carries the key's public half and the caller's name. That form is called a certificate signing request. The caller sends only that. On the other side sits the card zone's passport office. It is the certificate authority: the one the card zone trusts to stamp certificates. It checks the form. Is it well made? Does it carry the right name? Is that name on the card zone's list of callers? If so, it signs it, for a limited time. The signed certificate is good only for calling in, never for serving. Then one folder goes back to the caller. Inside are four things. The signed certificate. The office's own seal, so the caller can recognise the real card zone. A secret for the login server, which travels by its own secure channel. And the addresses to call. The caller checks the folder. It stores everything in its own Vault. Then it deletes the loose secret file. From then on, every call it makes carries two proofs: the certificate, and a token from the login server. No private key ever crosses between the two products. And every certificate is watched, so a warning goes out well before it expires. Now watch it happen. There is no portal for this, so watch the steps the bank's two operators see. On the caller's side, the installer makes the key and writes out one small form. The operator carries that form, and only that form, to the card zone's operator. The card zone's operator signs it. If the name on it is not a listed caller, the signing stops with a clear refusal, and nothing is issued. If the name is listed, a folder appears, named for that caller, with the four things inside. The operator carries the folder back. The secret goes separately, by a secure channel. On the caller's side, the installer reads the folder. It writes it all into the caller's Vault, and removes the secret file from disk. The first call then goes through the mail slot, and the card zone answers. Months later, the team sees a warning that the certificate is close to its end date. They repeat the same steps, and the old certificate retires on time. Nobody is woken by a sudden outage. What does this protect? Without this exchange, one product would hold another's secret. A leak on either side would expose both. With it, each product keeps its own key, in its own Vault. The card zone decides who may call, and for how long. The caller never touches the card zone's keys. That keeps the two products truly separate, sharing nothing. It also keeps the bank's audit simple: every certificate has one owner, one signer, and one end date. Back to the passport. A stamped photo goes through the post. The face never does.
