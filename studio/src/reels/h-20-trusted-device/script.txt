Pick up Aziz's phone and look at it. It is glass, a chip and a SIM card, and a million other phones look the same. So how does the platform know that this phone is Aziz's? In lesson nineteen, the platform linked Aziz to the bank's own customer records. This lesson looks at the thing in his hand: the device record, its states, the check at each request, and the app PIN. Go back to the day Aziz registered in the mobile bank. When his account was made, the platform also made a record for his phone. It gave the phone a random identifier. The app keeps this identifier and sends it with every request. The identifier is never made from the serial number or any other hardware value. It is random, so it tells nobody anything about the phone. Now look inside the record. It keeps the customer reference. It keeps Aziz's personal identification number, in a form that cannot be reversed. It keeps the platform, the model, the version of the system and the version of the app. It keeps the time the record was made, and the time the phone was last seen. And it keeps a state. One identifier belongs to one customer for its whole life. It never moves to a second person. On registration day, Aziz was at level one. So his phone got the state Known, not Trusted. Known means that the platform has seen this phone with this customer before. It says nothing about the person. A week later, Aziz passed MyID on this same phone, and his status became Confirmed. At that moment the record changed: the phone became Trusted. Only a MyID check that ends with Confirmed makes a device trusted. No code and no password can do it. Next, the app PIN. After the first login on a phone, the app asks Aziz to set an app PIN. The app is not usable until he does. The length is a policy of the bank. The app refuses easy PINs, like a simple run of digits, or one digit again and again. Here is the part I like most. The PIN never leaves the phone. The platform records only that a PIN was set, when, and on which device. Only the phone checks the PIN. The platform never accepts a PIN as proof of anything. And the PIN is not the password: the password opens a login, the PIN only unlocks the app on this phone. I think this split is honest. A short PIN is good for the hand, and weak for a server, so the server never trusts it. Now the threat. A stranger gets hold of Aziz's phone for a few minutes. He copies the app's tokens and puts them in the app on his own phone. His app sends a request with the copied tokens, and with its own device identifier. This is where the binding check works. At each request, the customer gateway asks three questions. First, is this device Trusted or Known? Second, does this device belong to the customer of this request? Third, is the personal identification number bound to this device the same as the customer's number? The stranger's phone fails the second question. It is not Aziz's device. One no is enough. The access grant ends at once. The device becomes Revoked, with the reason binding mismatch. The platform writes a security event, and the app must start a new login. A revoked device stays revoked. It never becomes trusted again. If someone logs in again on that same phone, the platform makes a new record, which starts from the beginning. An operator of the bank can also revoke one device, or all the devices of a customer, with a reason. The grant of a revoked device ends at its next request, not later. So the check runs on every request, not once a day. To recap: a random identifier, three states, one check at each request, and a PIN that never leaves the phone. Known means seen before. Trusted means MyID passed on this device. Revoked is final. But the binding check needs something to check. At each request, the app shows the gateway more than an identifier.
